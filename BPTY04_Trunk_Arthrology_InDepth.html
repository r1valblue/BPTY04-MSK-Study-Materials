1. The Intervertebral Disc: Structure, Function and Failure

The intervertebral disc is one of the most biomechanically sophisticated structures in the human body. It must simultaneously transmit compressive loads of several times bodyweight, resist torsional and shear forces, and permit the controlled multi-directional movement that makes the vertebral column functional. Understanding why and how the disc fails is inseparable from understanding how it is built.

Slide 5 – Nucleus pulposus composition

The Nucleus Pulposus: A Hydrostatic Pressure System

The nucleus pulposus (NP) occupies the central 40–50% of the disc by volume. Its gelatinous consistency arises from a high concentration of proteoglycans — large molecules with hydrophilic (water-attracting) side chains that draw water into the matrix by osmotic pressure. In youth, the NP contains approximately 90% water, but this figure falls progressively to around 70% with age (Antoniou et al. 1996). This dehydration is not merely a passive loss — it directly impairs the NP's ability to distribute compressive load hydrostatically, shifting more stress onto the peripheral annulus fibrosus and facet joints.

When an axial load is applied, the NP deforms and generates a hydrostatic pressure that is transmitted radially to the surrounding annulus fibrosus. This mechanism converts a compressive force into a tensile force across the annular fibres — a remarkably efficient load distribution strategy. When the NP degenerates and loses its water content, this conversion fails, and the annular fibres must bear axial loads they are not designed to resist, accelerating degeneration (Newell et al. 2017).

Slide 7 – Cartilaginous endplate structure

The Annulus Fibrosus: Lamellar Architecture and Tensile Resistance

The annulus fibrosus (AF) consists of 15–25 concentric lamellae, each oriented at approximately 65° to the vertical axis but alternating in direction between lamellae. This crossed-ply arrangement means that no matter which direction the trunk moves, approximately half of all lamellae are placed under tension — making the AF resistant to torsion in both directions simultaneously. The outer lamellae contain predominantly type I collagen (high tensile strength), while the inner lamellae transition to type II collagen (more compressive tolerance), blending into the nucleus pulposus.

The outer annulus is the only portion of the disc that receives a direct blood and nerve supply. This has two important clinical implications: first, tears of the outer annulus cause pain (discogenic pain); second, only the outer annulus has any real capacity for healing. Inner annular tears go largely unrepaired, progressing silently until they compromise disc structural integrity.

The Cartilaginous Endplate: Nutrition and Protection

The cartilaginous endplate (CEP) is a thin layer of hyaline cartilage covering the superior and inferior surfaces of each disc. Since the disc is avascular beyond early childhood, the CEP provides the primary route for nutrient diffusion and waste product removal by osmosis. Damage to the CEP — common in repetitive compressive loading — impairs this nutritional pathway and has been proposed as a key initiating factor in disc degeneration. The CEP also protects the vertebral body from being indented by the pressurised nucleus under high compressive loads.

Evidence: Physiotherapy for Disc-Related Low Back Pain A systematic review and meta-analysis (PMC7934127) found that physiotherapy interventions for lumbar prolapsed IVD are effective through several mechanisms: correction of disc displacement, opening of the intervertebral foramina, increase in intervertebral space, and reduction in herniation size via negative intradiscal pressure generated by certain loading positions. Extension-based exercises (McKenzie approach) reduce stress in the posterior annulus, suggesting a biomechanical rationale for their use in posterior disc herniation.

Disc Herniation: The Mechanism

Disc herniation occurs when the nucleus pulposus displaces through tears in the annulus fibrosus. The posterior annulus is thinner and less reinforced than the anterior annulus (the posterior longitudinal ligament is narrower than the ALL), which explains why posterolateral herniations are most common. The classic mechanism is sustained or repetitive flexion combined with compressive loading — for example, bending forward to lift a heavy object — which shifts the nucleus posteriorly, stressing the posterior annular fibres. A single episode of loading rarely causes herniation; it is more often the endpoint of cumulative microtrauma to the annular lamellae.

🏥 Clinical: Neurological Pattern of Disc Herniation The level of disc herniation determines which nerve root is compressed and therefore the neurological pattern. L4/L5 herniation typically compresses the L5 root (foot dorsiflexion weakness, numbness over dorsal foot). L5/S1 herniation compresses S1 (absent ankle reflex, plantar flexion weakness, numbness lateral foot/sole). Physiotherapy: directional preference exercises, traction, neurodynamic mobilisation, progressive functional loading.

2. Spinal Ligaments: Passive Stabilisers of the Column

The spinal ligaments form the passive subsystem of spinal stability, working alongside the active (muscular) and neural subsystems described in Panjabi's model of spinal stability (1992). Each ligament is positioned to resist specific directions of load, and their collective contribution is greatest at the end range of movement when the muscles are least mechanically advantaged.

Slide 11 – Anterior longitudinal ligament

Slide 12 – Posterior longitudinal ligament

Anterior and Posterior Longitudinal Ligaments

The anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) is the strongest spinal ligament. Running along the entire anterior vertebral column from the skull base to the sacrum, it is firmly attached to the vertebral bodies but only loosely to the intervertebral discs, creating a protective sliding arrangement that prevents disc bulge anteriorly while constraining extension. Its three-layer arrangement (deep, superficial, and collagenous) provides graded stiffness for different magnitudes of load.

The posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL) runs inside the spinal canal along the posterior vertebral bodies. It is significantly narrower than the ALL — particularly in the lumbar region where it widens only slightly at each disc level — which is why the posterolateral disc (just lateral to where the PLL ends) is the zone of least resistance and the most common site of herniation. The PLL limits flexion and provides some reinforcement to the posterior disc, but its reduced width at lumbar levels means that protection is incomplete.

The Ligamentum Flavum: Why It Is Unique

The ligamentum flavum is anatomically remarkable: it is the only true elastic ligament in the human body, containing approximately 80% elastin fibres compared to the collagen-dominated composition of all other spinal ligaments. This elasticity serves a specific mechanical purpose. When the spine flexes, the ligamentum flavum stretches; when the spine returns to neutral, it recoils, actively helping to restore the lordotic position without generating a passive buckling force into the spinal canal. If the ligamentum flavum were inelastic (like the supraspinous ligament), spinal flexion and extension would cause it to slacken and fold into the canal at neutral — a dangerous scenario. Its pre-tension at neutral ensures it remains taut throughout the range of motion.

🏥 Clinical: Ligamentum Flavum Hypertrophy and Spinal Stenosis With age and repetitive loading, the ligamentum flavum can hypertrophy and lose elasticity. It may buckle into the spinal canal during extension, contributing to lumbar spinal stenosis alongside osteophytes and disc bulging. Clinically, patients present with neurogenic claudication — bilateral leg pain/weakness that worsens with walking or standing (extension) and relieves with sitting or forward leaning (flexion). Distinguishing this from vascular claudication is important: neurogenic claudication relieves with sitting (flexion), while vascular claudication relieves with standing still.

Posterior Ligament Complex

The interspinous, supraspinous, and nuchal ligaments form the posterior ligament complex. They resist anterior shear and flexion by acting as a long lever arm behind the vertebral axis of rotation. The posterior ligament complex is commonly injured in hyperflexion trauma (seatbelt injuries, rugby tackles), and partial tears manifest as post-traumatic instability with pain specifically at end-range flexion. The nuchal ligament in the cervical spine is a specialised fibroelastic structure — more extensible than its thoracolumbar counterparts — that provides an attachment for the cervical extensor muscles while still limiting flexion.

Mnemonic: Which Ligament Limits What? ALL = Anticipates Leaning Length (limits extension — prevents over-leaning back)
PLL = Prevents Looking Low (limits flexion — prevents over-bending forward)
LF = Loves Flexion (limits flexion; elastic — stretches as you flex)
SSL = Stops Sliding Limbs (interspinous/supraspinous — limits flexion)

3. Zygapophyseal Joints: Regional Variation and Clinical Significance

The zygapophyseal joints (facet joints) are paired synovial joints between adjacent vertebral articular processes. Their orientation varies systematically between spinal regions — and this variation is not arbitrary. The orientation of the facet joints determines which movements are permitted, which are restricted, and how loads are shared between the disc and the facets. Together with the intervertebral disc, they form the three-joint complex at each spinal level, and dysfunction in any one component affects the other two.

Slide 14 – Regional facet orientation

Why Facet Orientation Matters

In the cervical spine, facets lie at approximately 45° to the horizontal, allowing motion in multiple planes — flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and rotation all occur readily. This permissiveness suits the functional demands of the head and neck but creates vulnerability to shear loading (explaining why cervical acceleration–deceleration injuries predominantly affect C4/5–C6/7). In the thoracic spine, the more vertical (coronal plane) facet orientation combined with the rib cage dramatically restricts axial rotation, providing a stable base for respiratory mechanics. In the lumbar spine, the sagittal (or slightly oblique) orientation of the facets allows large-amplitude flexion and extension but strongly resists rotation — the lumbar spine contributes less than 10° to total trunk rotation. This is clinically important: axial rotation under load (e.g., lifting and twisting simultaneously) forces the facets to accept shear loading they are not designed for, and high discal torsional stress results (PMC3705911).

Evidence: Facet Joint as Source of Pain Facet joints contain nociceptive fibres in their capsules, synovium, and subchondral bone. Biomechanical and clinical research (PMC3705911, PMC7002062) confirms that excessive compression or capsular strain activates these nociceptors. Facet joint pain is estimated to account for 15–45% of chronic low back pain and 54–67% of chronic neck pain. Diagnostic medial branch blocks are the gold standard for confirmation. Physiotherapy evidence (PubMed 23468052) supports soft tissue massage and lumbar mobility exercises for short-to-medium term relief.

Slide 15 – Articular capsule of facet joints

The Articular Capsule and Load Sharing

The articular capsule of the zygapophyseal joint is thin and fibrous, lined by synovial membrane. In the cervical spine, the capsule is notably lax to accommodate the large range of motion at these joints. In the lumbar spine, the capsule is tighter and contains specialised meniscoid inclusions — small synovial folds that can become trapped between articular surfaces, a proposed mechanism for some cases of acute locked back.

Under normal conditions, the facet joints transmit approximately 10–20% of axial compressive load through the spine. This proportion increases significantly with disc height loss (as occurs with degeneration) and during extension, when the facets impinge and the discs are unloaded. Understanding this load-sharing relationship explains why patients with severe disc degeneration often report increased pain in extension (greater facet loading) and relief in flexion (disc unloaded, facets gapped).

4. The Cervical Spine: Uncovertebral Joints and Upper Cervical Complex

The cervical spine contains several joint types found nowhere else in the vertebral column — a reflection of its unique functional demands. The head must orient to visual and auditory stimuli with rapid, precise, multi-planar movements while also housing and protecting the spinal cord, vertebral arteries, and the origin of multiple cranial nerves.

Slide 24 – Atlas-axis complex

Uncovertebral Joints: The Guides of Cervical Motion

The uncovertebral joints (Joints of Luschka) are present between C3–C7 and are formed by the uncinate processes — bony projections on the superolateral edges of the lower cervical vertebral bodies that fit into corresponding bevels on the vertebra above. Although their precise anatomical status is debated (some consider them fibrocartilaginous; others argue they are true synovial joints), their biomechanical role is clear: they act as lateral guides that prevent the vertebra from sliding sideways, and they limit lateral flexion in the cervical spine. This guiding function means that during axial rotation, lateral translation is minimised — an important protective mechanism for the disc and cord.

Osteophytic growth at the uncovertebral joints is extremely common with age and is often the anatomical basis of cervicogenic headache (osteophytes at C2/3, C3/4), cervical radiculopathy (foraminal encroachment), and vertebrobasilar symptoms (vertebral artery is adjacent to the uncovertebral joint from C2 to C6).

The Upper Cervical Complex: Specialisation for Head Movement

The upper cervical spine (occiput–C1–C2) is a highly specialised region that provides two distinct movement strategies. The atlanto-occipital joint produces nodding — pure flexion and extension of approximately 15° each — by virtue of the egg-shaped occipital condyles rocking in the concave superior facets of the atlas. Lateral flexion of approximately 5–8° is also possible here, but rotation is severely restricted by the geometry of the condyles.

Slide 27 – Alar ligaments

The atlanto-axial joint is specifically designed for rotation — it provides approximately 40–45° of rotation to each side, accounting for nearly half of total cervical rotation. This enormous rotational range is made possible by the flat articular surfaces of the lateral atlantoaxial joints and the unique pivot mechanism of the medial atlantoaxial joint, where the dens of the axis rotates within the osteoligamentous ring formed by the anterior arch of C1 and the transverse ligament.

Panjabi's Three-Column Model at the Craniocervical Junction The stability of the atlantoaxial articulation depends entirely on soft tissue — there is no bony locking mechanism. The transverse ligament of the atlas is the primary stabiliser of the dens against anterior displacement. It bears tensile forces of up to several hundred Newtons in everyday activities. The alar ligaments act as check-reins for rotation and lateral flexion, and their integrity must be assessed before high-velocity cervical manipulation.

🏥 Clinical: Upper Cervical Instability — Screening Before Manipulation Conditions associated with upper cervical instability include rheumatoid arthritis (transverse ligament erosion), Down syndrome (ligamentous laxity), trauma, and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. The Sharp-Purser Test screens for atlantoaxial instability by posterior translation of the head on the neck. The Transverse Ligament Stress Test (anterior shear) directly challenges the transverse ligament. Red flags include: bilateral or quadrilateral neurological symptoms, upper motor neuron signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski), dysphagia, drop attacks, or profound upper cervical pain following trauma. Positive screening tests are a contraindication to cervical manipulation (Physiopedia — Sharp-Purser Test; PMC9964642).

5. Costovertebral and Thoracic Joints: Mechanics of Breathing

The articulations of the thoracic cage form a mechanically elegant system that couples vertebral column movement with respiratory function. The ribs attach to the vertebral column posteriorly (via costovertebral and costotransverse joints) and to the sternum anteriorly (via sternocostal joints and costal cartilages), creating a semi-rigid ring whose dimensions change predictably with breathing.

Slide 37 – Costotransverse joints

Coupled Motion of Costovertebral and Costotransverse Joints

Rib elevation during inspiration requires a simultaneous twisting movement at the costovertebral joint and a rotation or gliding movement at the costotransverse joint. The two joints must act in concert — dysfunction at either impairs rib mobility and therefore respiratory mechanics. The upper ribs (1–6) move primarily in the pump-handle direction (anterior elevation, increasing AP diameter), while the lower ribs (7–10) move in the bucket-handle direction (lateral elevation, increasing transverse diameter). This regional distinction depends on the changing geometry of the costotransverse joint surfaces: the upper ribs have convex rib facets against concave transverse process facets (permitting rotation only), while the lower ribs have flat-on-flat facets (permitting gliding and rotation, producing the bucket-handle motion).

Sternum and Sternal Joints

The manubriosternal joint (secondary cartilaginous, symphysis type) is normally a fibrous union in adults, but it retains a thin fibrocartilaginous disc that allows a few degrees of flexion during deep inspiration. This small movement is amplified through the costal cartilages to increase thoracic volume. Importantly, the manubriosternal joint forms the Angle of Louis — the palpable horizontal ridge at the sternal angle — which is the clinical landmark for the 2nd rib, the carina of the trachea, and the aortic arch. The costal cartilages themselves absorb elastic energy during inspiration and return it during passive expiration, reducing the work of breathing.

🏥 Clinical: Thoracic Hypomobility and Respiratory Function Reduced mobility of the costovertebral and costotransverse joints — common in ankylosing spondylitis, prolonged immobilisation, or thoracic kyphosis — directly reduces respiratory capacity by limiting rib excursion. Chest expansion measurement (normal ≥5 cm at xiphisternum) reflects this. Physiotherapy: thoracic mobilisation, rib cage mobilisation, breathing pattern retraining, and inspiratory muscle training. In post-surgical or post-trauma patients, pain-limited breathing predisposes to atelectasis.

6. The Temporomandibular Joint

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is biomechanically unlike any other joint in the body. It is a synovial condyloid joint, but its articular surfaces are covered by fibrocartilage rather than hyaline cartilage (a feature shared only with the acromioclavicular and sternoclavicular joints among synovial joints), and it contains a complete intraarticular disc that molds itself to the geometry of the joint during movement. Furthermore, the two TMJs must always act in concert — isolated unilateral movement is anatomically impossible — making the system a paired bilateral synovial joint unique in the musculoskeletal system.

Slide 54 – TMJ intraarticular disc

The Articular Disc: A Dynamic Adaptor

The intraarticular disc divides the TMJ into two compartments. The lower compartment (between the condyle and the disc) permits rotation — the hinge movement of early mouth opening. The upper compartment (between the disc and the temporal bone) permits translation — the sliding movement that allows wider mouth opening and jaw protrusion. During normal opening, the condyle first rotates in the lower compartment, then translates anteriorly in the upper compartment while the disc moves with the condyle. Anterior disc displacement — where the disc slips anteriorly relative to the condyle — is the most common structural disorder of the TMJ. When the condyle recaptures the displaced disc during opening (the click recurs), this is called disc displacement with reduction; when it cannot recapture it, this is disc displacement without reduction (closed lock).

🏥 Clinical: TMJ Disorders and Physiotherapy TMJ disorders (TMD) affect approximately 5–12% of the population. They present as jaw pain (often referred to the ear or temple), limited mouth opening, clicking or crepitus, and headache. Physiotherapy treatment includes: joint mobilisation of the condyle and disc, masticatory muscle treatment (massage and dry needling to masseter, temporalis, pterygoids), postural correction of the craniocervical region (strong evidence for cervical contribution to TMD), splint therapy, and patient education on parafunctional habits. Evidence supports physiotherapy as equivalent to splint therapy for pain and function.
