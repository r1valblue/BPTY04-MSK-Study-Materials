1. Intervertebral Discs

Intervertebral discs (IVDs) separate the vertebral bodies from C2 to S1. They are pads of fibrocartilage that allow the vertebral column to bend and twist (Bogduk 2005). Each disc has three main components: the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilaginous endplate.

Slide 4 – IVD overview and three components

Slide 9 – Cross-section of disc anatomy

Nucleus Pulposus (NP)

  • Gelatinous central structure (40–50% of disc volume)
  • Composed of: proteoglycan (35–65%), type II collagen (5–20%), water
  • High water content: deforms under hydrostatic pressure; generates pressure in annulus fibrosus
  • Water content decreases with age: 90% → 70% (Antoniou et al. 1996)

Annulus Fibrosus (AF)

  • 15–25 concentric layers (lamellae)
  • Composition: Proteoglycan (20%), Collagen (50–70%), Elastin (2%)
  • Outer zone: Type I collagen — high tensile strength; connects marginal ridges of adjacent vertebrae
  • Inner zone: Type II collagen — fibrocartilaginous; provides compressive strength

Slide 5 – Nucleus pulposus composition

Slide 6 – Annulus fibrosus layers

Cartilaginous Endplate (CEP)

  • Thin layers of hyaline cartilage binding the disc superiorly and inferiorly
  • Composed of: 60% water, type II collagen, proteoglycans
  • Permits osmosis of nutrients into the disc (avascular structure)
  • Protects the vertebra from pressure
  • Under compression: NP presses against the CEP and bony endplates

Slide 7 – Cartilaginous endplate

Slide 10 – Disc degeneration and hernia

Functions of the IVD

  • Acts as a "water cushion" to absorb transient axial load
  • Resilient under pressure
  • Under load: diminution of nucleus height; annulus fibers are braced and prevented from buckling
🏥 Clinical: Disc Herniation When the nucleus pulposus severely degenerates, it loses its ability to cushion load. This can result in herniation — the nucleus protrudes through the annulus fibrosus, potentially compressing adjacent nerve roots (Humphreys and Eck 1999). Most common at L4/L5 and L5/S1.

2. Vertebral Body Ligaments

Slide 12 – Posterior longitudinal ligament

LigamentAttachmentLayers / NotesRole
Anterior Longitudinal Ligament (ALL) Anterior surface of vertebral bodies and IVDs — skull base to sacrum 3 layers: deep (adjacent bodies), superficial (spans multiple), collagenous (firm to body, loose to disc) Limits extension; reinforces IVD
Posterior Longitudinal Ligament (PLL) Posterior surface of vertebral bodies and IVDs — clivus to sacral canal Narrow and weaker than ALL — contributes to disc herniation risk posterolaterally Limits flexion; reinforces IVD
Key Point The ALL limits extension and the PLL limits flexion. Think: ALL = All the way forward on extension; PLL = Posterior = restricts going forward (flexion).

3. Zygapophyseal (Facet) Joints

Paired facet joints formed by the articular processes of adjacent vertebrae: the inferior articular facets of the superior vertebra articulate with the superior articular facets (concave) of the inferior vertebra.

Slide 13 – Facet joint anatomy

Slide 14 – Regional orientation of facets

RegionFacet ShapeSuperior Facet FacesInferior Facet FacesMovement Implication
Cervical Flat, oval — oblique plane Upward and medially Downward, forwards, laterally Allows flexion, extension, rotation
Thoracic Thin, less triangular — vertical Backwards and laterally Forwards and medially Allows rotation; limits flexion (rib cage)
Lumbar Strong — upward/downward projection Concave, medially and backwards Convex, forwards and laterally Allows flexion/extension; limits rotation

Slide 15 – Articular capsule of zygapophyseal joints

Articular Capsule

  • Each joint surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule inserted into the articular facet margins
  • Attached to the ligamentum flavum
  • Cervical: broad and lax (favours gliding)
  • Thoracic and Lumbar: narrower
  • Role: Prevents extreme sliding movements of vertebrae
🏥 Clinical: Facet Joint Syndrome Zygapophyseal joint degeneration (facet arthropathy) is a significant source of axial back and neck pain. The cervical facets are commonly involved in whiplash and the lumbar facets in degenerative low back pain. Physiotherapy: joint mobilisation, stabilisation exercises, postural correction.

4. Uncovertebral Joints (Joints of Luschka)

Slide 16 – Uncovertebral joint anatomy

  • Also called Luschka joints — small synovial joints between the five lower cervical vertebral bodies (C3–C7)
  • Located between the lateral parts of adjacent vertebral bodies
  • Lateral edges of the inferior vertebra are lipped to fit the bevelled edges of the vertebra above
  • Each joint surrounded by a capsule continuous medially with the IVD
  • Allow flexion and extension; limit lateral flexion in the cervical spine
  • Role: Control movements and stabilise the neck
Key Point Uncovertebral joints are unique to the cervical spine. Osteophytes forming here can compress the vertebral artery or nerve roots — a common cause of cervicogenic headache and cervical radiculopathy.

5. Joints Between Adjacent Laminae — Ligamentum Flavum

Slide 17 – Ligamentum flavum

  • Thick and powerful ligament — the only true elastic ligament of the body
  • Yellowish colour due to high elastin fibre content
  • Attachment: Between laminae of 2 adjacent vertebrae (right and left pairs)
  • Role: Preserve spinal curvature
  • When spine is erect: under tension — assists back muscles to stabilise the spine
  • Limits forward flexion
🏥 Clinical: Ligamentum Flavum Hypertrophy With age, the ligamentum flavum can buckle and hypertrophy, contributing to lumbar spinal stenosis. This narrows the spinal canal and may compress the cauda equina, causing neurogenic claudication.

6. Joints Between Transverse Processes — Intertransverse Ligaments

Slide 18 – Intertransverse ligament

  • Attachment: Between the transverse processes of 2 adjacent vertebrae
  • Role: Limit rocking movement of vertebra upon another; limit lateral flexion of the trunk
  • Possibly absent in the cervical spine; often replaced by intertransverse muscles
  • More obvious in the lumbar spine

8. Atlas & Axis Joints

The craniocervical junction comprises 5 joints connecting the skull, atlas (C1), and axis (C2).

Slide 24 – Overview of 5 atlas-axis joints

Slide 25 – Cruciform ligament

Atlanto-Occipital Joint

  • Paired joints between the superior concave articular facets of the atlas and the convex condyles of the occiput
  • Main movement: nodding (flexion/extension) — ~15° each direction

Atlanto-Axial Joint

  • Lateral (paired): Between inferior concave articular facets of atlas and superior facets of axis
  • Medial (unpaired): Between the dens of the axis and the cartilage-covered anterior surface of the transverse ligament of the atlas
  • Main movement: rotation — ~40–45° each side (responsible for ~50% of total cervical rotation)

9. Costovertebral & Costotransverse Joints

Slide 34 – Costovertebral joints

Slide 37 – Costotransverse joints

Costovertebral Joints (Joints of the Head of the Ribs)

  • Ribs 2–9: articulate with the demi-facets of two adjacent vertebral bodies; convex rib head facet + concave costal facets; intraarticular ligament divides joint cavity in two
  • Ribs 1, 10, 11, 12: articulate with only one vertebra; single joint cavity; poorly developed radiate ligament
LigamentAttachmentRole
Radiate ligament of head Head of rib → vertebral bodies + IVD of 2 adjacent vertebrae Reinforces the joint anteriorly
Intraarticular ligament Crest of rib head → IVD Divides joint cavity into 2 parts; stabilises joint

Slide 35 – Radiate ligament of head

Slide 36 – Intraarticular ligament

Costotransverse Joints (Ribs 1–10)

  • Articular facet on costal tubercle of rib ↔ costal fovea on transverse process
  • Upper part (ribs 1–6): rib facet convex, transverse process concave → rotation only
  • Lower part (ribs 7–10): both facets flat → gliding + rotation

Common Movements at Costovertebral/Costotransverse Joints

  • As the rib is raised or lowered: twisting and gliding at costovertebral joints — limited by radiate and intraarticular ligaments
  • At costotransverse joints: upper ribs → rotation only; lower ribs → gliding + rotation

Clinical Landmark: Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis) The manubriosternal joint creates the sternal angle, palpable as a horizontal ridge. It marks the attachment of the 2nd rib — the starting point for counting ribs clinically. Also marks the carina of the trachea and the aortic arch.

11. Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ)

Slide 53 – TMJ overview

Slide 54 – TMJ intraarticular disc

  • Connects the jaw bone (mandible) to the skull (temporal bone)
  • One of the few articulations containing a complete intraarticular disc that molds itself during movements
  • Synovial condyloid joint between:
    • Mandibular fossa of the temporal bone
    • Corresponding condyle of the mandible
🏥 Clinical: TMJ Dysfunction TMJ disorders (pain, clicking, locking) are common and often involve disc displacement. Physiotherapy includes jaw mobilisation, postural correction, and masticatory muscle treatment.
