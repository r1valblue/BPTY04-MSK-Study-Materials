🏥 Clinical: Scalene and SCM The scalene muscles form the borders of the scalene triangle through which the brachial plexus and subclavian artery pass — tightness or hypertrophy can cause thoracic outlet syndrome. The SCM is innervated by CN XI (spinal accessory nerve) — assessing its strength tests CN XI integrity. Torticollis (wry neck) involves SCM spasm or contracture, causing ipsilateral lateral flexion and contralateral rotation of the head.

5. Erector Spinae Group

The erector spinae is a large muscle group divided into 3 sub-groups from lateral to medial: Iliocostalis → Longissimus → Spinalis.

Common origin: A common tendon (thoracolumbar aponeurosis) attached to the posterior surface of the sacrum, iliac crest, and spinous processes of lumbar and lower two thoracic vertebrae.

Insertions: Various — posterior ribs, spinous and transverse processes of thoracic and cervical vertebrae, mastoid process of temporal bone.

🏥 Clinical: Multifidus in Low Back Pain The multifidus is the most important deep spinal stabiliser. Research consistently shows multifidus atrophy and delayed activation in chronic low back pain patients. Segmental multifidus activation (co-contraction with transversus abdominis) is a cornerstone of spinal stabilisation physiotherapy.

Abdominal Wall Rotation Rule Obliquus externus rotates contralaterally (fibres run downward-forward like reaching into opposite pocket). Obliquus internus rotates ipsilaterally. For right trunk rotation: RIGHT obliquus internus + LEFT obliquus externus work together — opposite-side obliques always pair for rotation.
🏥 Clinical: Core Stability and Transversus Abdominis Transversus abdominis (TrA) is the deepest abdominal muscle and the primary contributor to intra-abdominal pressure and spinal stiffness. Studies show TrA activates prior to limb movements in healthy subjects but is delayed in LBP patients. "Drawing-in" manoeuvre (gently pulling navel toward spine) preferentially activates TrA — the starting point for spinal stabilisation protocols.

10. Diaphragm

Slide 72 – Diaphragm origins

Slide 73 – Diaphragm insertion and innervation

Diaphragm Structure

The diaphragm has three parts:

  • Sternal part: posterior surface of xiphoid process
  • Costal part: inner surface of 7th–12th ribs
  • Lumbar part:
    • Medial: vertebral bodies L1–L3, 2nd–3rd IVD, anterior longitudinal ligament
    • Lateral (3 tendinous arches): 1st = L1; 2nd = vertebral body to costal process L2; 3rd = costal process L2 to 12th rib
PropertyDetail
InsertionCentral tendon
ActionPrimary muscle of respiration — flattens on contraction, increasing thoracic volume (inspiration)
NerveN. phrenicus from cervical plexus C3–C5
Phrenic Nerve: "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive" Injury at C3 or above = complete respiratory paralysis. Injury at C5/C6 = preserved diaphragm function only. Important for spinal cord injury prognosis.
🏥 Clinical: Diaphragm as Postural Stabiliser Beyond respiration, the diaphragm contributes to spinal stability by generating intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) together with the pelvic floor and transversus abdominis. Dysfunctional breathing patterns (e.g., upper chest breathing) impair both respiratory efficiency and the IAP contribution to lumbar stability — explaining why breathing pattern disorders are assessed in chronic low back pain patients.
