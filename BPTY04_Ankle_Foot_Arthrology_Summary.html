1. Ankle & Foot Movements Overview

Slide 9 – Functional joints overview

Slide 10 – Movement planes and axes

The ankle and foot complex has 33 joints in total. The functional joints involved in movement are:

  • Talocrural (ankle): dorsiflexion / plantarflexion
  • Subtalar: inversion / eversion
  • Transverse tarsal (midtarsal): pronation / supination contribution
  • Intertarsal: foot mobility
  • Tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc): foot mobility
  • Metatarsophalangeal (MTP): flexion/extension, abduction/adduction
  • Interphalangeal (IP): flexion/extension
MovementPlaneAxis
Eversion / InversionFrontalSagittal
Dorsiflexion / PlantarflexionSagittalFrontal
Abduction / AdductionTransverseVertical
Pronation vs Supination Pronation = eversion + dorsiflexion + abduction (combined triplanar motion)
Supination = inversion + plantarflexion + adduction
Note: The convex-concave rule does NOT apply to the subtalar joint due to its complex multiplanar axis.

2. Interosseous Membrane

Slide 11 – Interosseous membrane

The interosseous membrane (IOM) of the leg is a fibrous sheet connecting the interosseous borders of the tibia and fibula along the length of the leg.

  • Fibre direction: run distally and laterally from the tibial border to the fibula
  • Function: transmits forces between tibia and fibula; resists widening of the interosseous space; provides muscular attachment for the deep anterior and posterior compartment muscles
  • Opening: a gap at the proximal end allows the anterior tibial vessels to pass to the anterior compartment
Force Transmission The IOM transmits approximately 1/6 of body weight from fibula to tibia during weight-bearing, supplementing the primary load path through the ankle mortise. This is clinically relevant in fibular fractures — even isolated fibular shaft fractures can affect ankle stability if the IOM is disrupted.

3. Distal Tibiofibular Syndesmosis

Slide 12 – Syndesmosis

Slide 13 – Tibiofibular joints

The distal tibiofibular syndesmosis is a fibrous joint (syndesmosis type) between the fibular notch of the tibia and the distal fibula. It is essential for maintaining the integrity of the ankle mortise.

Stabilising Structures

LigamentPositionRole
Anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL)AnteriorPrimary restraint; most commonly torn in syndesmotic sprain
Posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL)PosteriorStrongest syndesmotic ligament
Inferior transverse tibiofibular ligamentDeep posteriorForms posterior labrum of ankle joint
Interosseous ligamentWithin jointContinuation of IOM; resists diastasis
🏥 Clinical: High Ankle Sprain (Syndesmotic Sprain) Injury to the syndesmosis ("high ankle sprain") typically results from external rotation of the foot with the ankle dorsiflexed. The AITFL is torn first. Symptoms: pain above the ankle at the anterior syndesmosis, positive squeeze test (fibular compression at mid-shaft reproduces distal pain), positive external rotation stress test. Recovery is significantly slower than lateral ankle sprain (6–12+ weeks vs 4–6 weeks) due to the need to restore mortise integrity.

4. Tibiofibular Joints

JointTypeArticular SurfacesMovementsStabilisers
Proximal tibiofibular joint Plane synovial Fibular head and lateral tibial condyle Small gliding; fibula moves superiorly during dorsiflexion Anterior and posterior tibiofibular ligaments; fibular collateral ligament
Distal tibiofibular joint (syndesmosis) Fibrous (syndesmosis) Fibular notch of tibia and distal fibula Slight widening during dorsiflexion to accommodate wider talar dome AITFL, PITFL, inferior transverse ligament, interosseous ligament
Ankle Mortise Widening During dorsiflexion, the wider anterior talar trochlea forces the fibula to move laterally (~1–2 mm) and rotate slightly — the syndesmosis allows this and then recoils. Disruption of the syndesmosis allows pathological widening of the mortise, reducing joint contact area and predisposing to chondral damage.

5. Talocrural (Ankle) Joint

Slide 14 – Ankle joint

Slide 15 – Talocrural anatomy

PropertyDetail
Joint typeSynovial hinge joint (ginglymus)
Articular surfacesInferior surface of tibia (plafond) + medial malleolus + lateral malleolus (mortise) ↔ superior surface of talar body (trochlea tali)
Primary movementsDorsiflexion (0–20°) / Plantarflexion (0–50°)
CapsuleThin anteriorly and posteriorly; reinforced medially (deltoid) and laterally (ATFL, CFL, PTFL)

Talar Trochlea Shape

The talar dome is wider anteriorly than posteriorly by approximately 3–6 mm. This has critical biomechanical implications:

  • Dorsiflexion: wider anterior dome wedges into the mortise → bony stability, lateral ligaments relatively slack
  • Plantarflexion: narrower posterior dome in the mortise → less bony congruence, ankle ligamentously dependent → vulnerable to sprains
🏥 Clinical: Why Lateral Ankle Sprains Occur in Plantarflexion The combined position of plantarflexion (narrower talar dome in mortise) + inversion (ATFL aligned vertically and under tension) is the classic sprain mechanism. The ATFL becomes the primary restraint in this position and is the first ligament torn. This position is common in sport (landing from a jump, cutting) and explains why ankle sprains are the most common sports injury.

6. Subtalar (Talocalcaneal) Joint

Slide 16 – Subtalar joint

Slide 17 – Subtalar anatomy

PropertyDetail
Joint typeSynovial (plane/modified) — 3 articular facets
Articular surfacesThree facets between inferior talus and superior calcaneus (anterior, middle, posterior); separated by sinus tarsi
MovementsTriplanar motion about Manter's oblique axis: inversion/eversion, internal/external rotation, plantarflexion/dorsiflexion component
Key ligamentsInterosseous talocalcaneal ligament (in sinus tarsi — primary); cervical ligament; lateral talocalcaneal ligament
Sinus Tarsi The sinus tarsi is a tunnel-like space between the neck of the talus and the calcaneus, containing the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament, fat pad, and neurovascular structures. Sinus tarsi syndrome presents as lateral hindfoot pain with instability feeling following ankle sprain — caused by disruption of the sinus tarsi contents and proprioceptive structures.

7. Transverse Tarsal Joint (Chopart's Joint)

Slide 18 – Transverse tarsal joint

The transverse tarsal (midtarsal) joint — also called Chopart's joint — is a functional joint formed by two component articulations running across the foot:

  • Talonavicular joint (medial): between head of talus and proximal navicular; ball-and-socket type
  • Calcaneocuboid joint (lateral): between anterior calcaneus and posterior cuboid; saddle type

Axis Theory (Elftman)

PositionAxes alignmentEffect
Pronation (eversion)Axes diverge (oblique)Joint unlocked → foot mobile → shock absorption
Supination (inversion)Axes become parallelJoint locked → foot rigid → propulsion platform
🏥 Clinical: Chopart Amputation Surgical disarticulation through the transverse tarsal joint — preserves the hindfoot but removes the midfoot and forefoot. The calcaneus and talus remain. Requires careful balance of remaining muscles to prevent equinus (plantarflexion) deformity. Named after French surgeon François Chopart.

8. Metatarsophalangeal & Interphalangeal Joints

Slide 19 – Metatarsophalangeal joints

Slide 20 – Interphalangeal joints

JointTypeMovementsClinical Note
Metatarsophalangeal (MTP) Condyloid synovial Flexion/extension + abduction/adduction 1st MTP requires ~65° dorsiflexion for normal terminal stance (windlass mechanism). Hallux valgus: lateral deviation of 1st MTP.
Proximal IP (PIP) Hinge synovial Flexion / extension only Hammer toe: PIP flexion contracture (most common at 2nd toe)
Distal IP (DIP) Hinge synovial Flexion / extension only Mallet toe: DIP flexion contracture
Tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc) Joint The TMT joint complex connects the distal tarsal row (3 cuneiforms + cuboid) to the 5 metatarsal bases. The 2nd metatarsal base sits recessed between the medial and lateral cuneiforms (the "keystone"). The Lisfranc ligament (medial cuneiform → base of 2nd MT) is the primary stabiliser. Injury: often missed on non-weight-bearing X-ray; weight-bearing views reveal gap >2 mm between 1st and 2nd MT bases. Untreated → post-traumatic midfoot arthritis.

9. Lateral Ankle Ligaments

Slide 21 – Lateral ligaments overview

Slide 22 – Lateral ligaments detail

Three distinct ligaments form the lateral collateral complex of the ankle:

LigamentOriginInsertionFunctionInjury frequency
ATFL (Anterior talofibular) Anterior border of lateral malleolus Lateral talar body (anterior) Resists anterior talar subluxation; primary restraint in plantarflexion + inversion Most commonly injured (~85% of lateral sprains)
CFL (Calcaneofibular) Tip of lateral malleolus Lateral calcaneus (tubercle) Resists inversion at both talocrural and subtalar; primary restraint in neutral/dorsiflexion Torn with ATFL in ~50–75% of Grade II/III sprains
PTFL (Posterior talofibular) Posterior lateral malleolus (malleolar fossa) Posterior talar process (lateral tubercle) Resists posterior talar translation; strongest of the three Rarely torn in isolation; seen in dislocations
Lateral Ligament Order: "All Cats Play" ATFL → CFL → PTFL
Torn in order from anterior to posterior as severity increases (Grade I = ATFL only; Grade III = all three).
🏥 Clinical: Ankle Sprain Grading Grade I: ATFL stretch — mild swelling, full weight-bearing, no instability
Grade II: ATFL tear ± CFL stretch — moderate swelling, antalgic gait, slight laxity on anterior drawer
Grade III: ATFL + CFL + ± PTFL tear — significant swelling, cannot weight-bear, positive anterior drawer + talar tilt tests
Management: POLICE (Protection, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation) acutely; proprioception and peroneal strengthening in rehabilitation.

10. Medial Ankle Ligaments — Deltoid Complex

Slide 23 – Deltoid/medial ligament complex

The deltoid ligament (medial collateral ligament) is a triangular, fan-shaped complex consisting of superficial and deep layers:

LayerLigamentOriginInsertionFunction
Superficial Tibionavicular Anterior medial malleolus Navicular (dorsal surface) Resists abduction/eversion; stabilises talonavicular joint
Tibiocalcaneal Medial malleolus Sustentaculum tali of calcaneus Resists eversion; supports subtalar joint medially
Superficial tibiotalar Medial malleolus Medial talus (posterior) Resists plantarflexion + eversion
Deep Anterior tibiotalar Anterior medial malleolus Medial talar body Primary restraint to external talar rotation and eversion
Posterior tibiotalar Posterior medial malleolus Medial talar body (posterior) Strongest component; primary restraint to eversion
🏥 Clinical: Why Medial Sprains Are Rare The deltoid complex is significantly stronger than the lateral ligaments. A forced eversion mechanism is more likely to avulse the medial malleolus (bony injury) than tear the deltoid. Isolated deltoid tears are rare and often indicate a more complex injury (e.g., in conjunction with a fibular fracture in a pronation-external rotation mechanism — the Maisonneuve fracture pattern).

11. Plantar Fascia

Slide 24 – Plantar fascia

PropertyDetail
StructureThick aponeurosis of dense connective tissue; three bands: central (largest), medial, lateral
OriginMedial calcaneal tubercle (plantar surface)
InsertionPlantar plates of MTP joints and bases of proximal phalanges (toes 1–5)
FunctionSupports the medial longitudinal arch; windlass mechanism during push-off

Windlass Mechanism

During terminal stance, dorsiflexion of the MTP joints wraps the plantar fascia around the metatarsal heads, shortening its effective length. This raises the medial longitudinal arch and converts the foot from a flexible, shock-absorbing structure into a rigid lever for propulsion. The windlass mechanism is essential for efficient push-off and energy return in walking and running.

🏥 Clinical: Plantar Fasciitis The most common cause of heel pain in active adults. Insertional microtrauma at the medial calcaneal tubercle → degenerative changes (not primarily inflammatory despite the name). Risk factors: prolonged standing, obesity, tight Achilles/calf complex, high BMI, sudden increase in activity load. Pain: sharp on first steps in the morning (post-static dyskinesia), easing with movement then worsening with sustained load. Management: load management, calf stretching, intrinsic foot strengthening, orthoses; shockwave therapy for recalcitrant cases (>6 months).
