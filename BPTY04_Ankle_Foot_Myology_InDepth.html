1. Functional Overview of Leg & Foot Musculature

The muscles acting on the ankle and foot are among the most biomechanically important in the body — responsible for absorbing forces up to 8× body weight during running, providing dynamic arch support, enabling fine metatarsophalangeal control, and generating the propulsive force of push-off. They are organised into four fascial compartments of the leg plus the intrinsic foot muscles, each with its own nerve supply, vascular system, and functional role.

A key distinction exists between the extrinsic muscles (originating in the leg, acting distally via long tendons crossing the ankle) and the intrinsic muscles (originating and inserting within the foot itself). Extrinsic muscles provide the gross power for ankle and toe movement; intrinsic muscles provide fine motor control of toe alignment, contribute to dynamic arch support, and stabilise the metatarsophalangeal joints during the propulsive phase of gait.

Functional note: Electromyographic studies confirm that both intrinsic and extrinsic foot muscles are active throughout the stance phase of gait, with intrinsics becoming particularly critical in the late stance / push-off phase as the windlass mechanism tensions the plantar fascia and the longitudinal arch must be maintained under load.[1]

2. Anterior Compartment — Dorsiflexors & Toe Extensors

The anterior compartment contains four muscles — tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus (EHL), extensor digitorum longus (EDL), and fibularis tertius — all innervated by the deep fibular (peroneal) nerve (L4–L5). The common fibular nerve wraps around the fibular neck and divides here into deep and superficial branches; the fibular neck is the most vulnerable point for traction/compression injury to the common fibular nerve in the lower limb.

Tibialis Anterior

The most powerful dorsiflexor, tibialis anterior also inverts the foot in the sagittal and frontal planes. Its origin spans the lateral tibial condyle and the upper two-thirds of the lateral tibial shaft plus the interosseous membrane; it inserts on the plantar surface of the medial cuneiform and the base of the first metatarsal. This distal insertion on the plantar-medial aspect means that activation inverts as well as dorsiflexes. During the swing phase of gait, tibialis anterior dorsiflexes the foot to achieve toe clearance — its failure (foot drop) is the cardinal sign of common fibular nerve palsy.

EHL and EDL

EHL originates from the middle fibula and inserts into the distal phalanx of the hallux; EDL originates more proximally and fans out to the dorsal digital expansions of toes 2–5. Both assist dorsiflexion secondarily. At the ankle, all three anterior tendons pass under the superior extensor retinaculum and the Y-shaped inferior extensor retinaculum, which prevent bowstringing. The order medial to lateral at the ankle joint is: tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL (with fibularis tertius most lateral).

Clinical — Foot Drop: Paralysis of the deep fibular nerve (commonly at the fibular neck from compression, fracture, or traction) causes foot drop — inability to dorsiflex and toe-clear during swing phase. Patients compensate with a high-stepping "steppage gait." Urgent assessment is required to exclude compartment syndrome, which can damage the anterior compartment acutely. Orthoses (ankle-foot orthoses, AFO) are the mainstay of conservative management.

3. Lateral Compartment — Evertors

The lateral compartment contains fibularis (peroneus) longus and fibularis (peroneus) brevis, both innervated by the superficial fibular nerve (L5–S1). Their shared primary action is eversion of the foot; they also contribute to plantarflexion. Both tendons pass posterior to the lateral malleolus within a fibro-osseous groove, held in place by the superior fibular retinaculum proximally and the inferior fibular retinaculum distally.

Fibularis Longus and Brevis — Functional Distinction

Fibularis brevis inserts into the tuberosity at the base of the 5th metatarsal and is the more direct lateral stabiliser of the rearfoot. Fibularis longus follows a more complex course — after passing the lateral malleolus, it curves under the cuboid through the fibular (peroneal) groove, then crosses the plantar aspect of the foot obliquely to insert on the plantar surface of the medial cuneiform and the base of the first metatarsal. This unique trajectory means fibularis longus acts as a dynamic support of both the lateral and transverse arches, as well as acting as an evertor.

Evidence — Fibularis tendons in ankle instability: Peroneal tendon subluxation or dislocation occurs when the superior fibular retinaculum is damaged — most commonly during acute lateral ankle inversion sprain. Up to 78% of peroneal tendon subluxations occur alongside lateral ligament sprains, making them one of the most frequently missed co-pathologies in acute ankle injuries. MRI or dynamic ultrasound are required to confirm retinacular integrity when peroneal snapping or subluxation is suspected.[2]

Fibularis Brevis Avulsion Fracture

A sudden inversion force can avulse the fibularis brevis insertion at the styloid process of the 5th metatarsal. This "pseudo-Jones" fracture at the base of the 5th metatarsal must be distinguished from a true Jones fracture (at the 5th metatarsal diaphysis–metaphysis junction), which has a poorer blood supply and higher non-union rate. Ottawa Foot Rules use the styloid process as a palpation point to guide imaging decisions.

4. Superficial Posterior Compartment — Triceps Surae

The superficial posterior compartment contains the three muscles of the Triceps Surae: gastrocnemius, soleus, and plantaris. All are innervated by the tibial nerve (S1–S2) and insert via the Achilles (calcaneal) tendon into the posterior calcaneal tuberosity. Together they are the dominant plantarflexors and play a central role in gait, posture, and propulsion.

Gastrocnemius vs. Soleus — Key Distinction

The most clinically important distinction between these two muscles is their biarticularity. Gastrocnemius crosses both the knee and ankle, originating from the posterior femoral condyles, which means its plantarflexion effectiveness depends heavily on knee position — it produces greater force with the knee extended and is less effective with the knee flexed (when it is shortened at its proximal end). Soleus, by contrast, originates from the proximal posterior tibia and fibula (monoarticular) and is effective at plantarflexion regardless of knee position.

Soleus is composed predominantly of slow-twitch (Type I) muscle fibres, making it a key postural muscle — it works continuously to prevent forward ankle collapse during standing (opposing gravity during quiet stance). Gastrocnemius has a higher proportion of fast-twitch (Type II) fibres and is more active during rapid propulsive activities such as running and jumping.

Evidence (JOSPT 2024 CPG): The 2024 JOSPT Clinical Practice Guideline for midportion Achilles tendinopathy recommends tendon loading exercises at the highest load tolerated as first-line treatment. A 2022 Sports Medicine – Open controlled trial demonstrated that 12 weeks of high-load strength training produced significant increases in tendon stiffness and cross-sectional area in Achilles tendinopathy, compared to isometric exercise alone — supporting progressive heavy-load resistance as the cornerstone of rehabilitation.[3, 4]

Achilles Tendon — Structure and Vulnerability

The Achilles tendon is the largest and strongest tendon in the body, capable of bearing forces of 6–8× body weight during running. It forms from the convergence of the gastrocnemius and soleus aponeuroses, rotating approximately 90° as it descends — placing fibres from the gastrocnemius medially at the calcaneal insertion and soleus fibres laterally. A zone of relative hypovascularity exists approximately 2–6 cm above the calcaneal insertion, corresponding to the most common site of both midportion tendinopathy and spontaneous tendon rupture. Rupture most commonly occurs during an eccentric plantarflexion mechanism (landing, pushing off) in middle-aged recreational athletes.

5. Deep Posterior Compartment

The deep posterior compartment contains four muscles: popliteus, tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus (FDL), and flexor hallucis longus (FHL). All are innervated by the tibial nerve (L4–S2). Their tendons pass through the tarsal tunnel (posterior to the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum), alongside the posterior tibial artery, veins, and tibial nerve itself.

Tarsal Tunnel — Anatomy and Clinical Relevance

The tarsal tunnel transmits (from anterior to posterior): Tibialis Posterior, FDL, the posterior tibial Artery and veins, the tibial Nerve, and FHL — recalled by the mnemonic "Tom, Dick And a Nervous Harry." Tibial nerve compression within this tunnel produces tarsal tunnel syndrome (analogous to carpal tunnel syndrome) — burning, tingling, and numbness along the plantar foot. Contributing factors include space-occupying lesions, varicose veins, pronated foot posture, and post-traumatic adhesions.

Tibialis Posterior and the Medial Longitudinal Arch

Tibialis posterior is the primary dynamic invertor of the subtalar joint and the most important muscular support of the medial longitudinal arch. Its broad distal insertion — primarily the navicular tuberosity with additional slips to all three cuneiforms, the cuboid, and the 2nd–4th metatarsal bases — means it acts like a "suspension cable" for the entire midfoot.

Evidence (PMC5620404, PMC4094099): Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD), now termed Progressive Collapsing Foot Deformity (PCFD), is the most common cause of adult-acquired flatfoot deformity, affecting up to 10% of adults over 40 and predominantly women. Stage I (tenosynovitis with intact arch) responds well to orthotics and eccentric strengthening; Stage II (flexible flatfoot) may require orthotics plus surgical tendon augmentation; Stage III (rigid fixed deformity) often requires arthrodesis.[5, 6]

Flexor Hallucis Longus — The "Functional Achilles"

FHL originates from the lower two-thirds of the posterior fibula and inserts into the base of the distal phalanx of the hallux. Despite its modest cross-sectional area, FHL is the key muscle for the final push-off force generation during walking and running — the hallux must flex powerfully at toe-off to propel the body forward. For this reason, FHL is sometimes called the "functional Achilles" of the foot.

FHL passes through its own synovial sheath within the tarsal tunnel, then under the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus, and through a fibro-osseous tunnel between the sesamoids beneath the first metatarsal head. At the "knot of Henry" (master knot — beneath the navicular), FHL and FDL cross and connect via a fibrous slip. FHL pathology — tenosynovitis or posterior ankle impingement involving its sheath — is a classic injury in ballet dancers who repeatedly work in extreme plantarflexion (pointe).

Clinical — FHL Tendinopathy in Dancers: A systematic review of posterior ankle impingement syndrome and FHL tendinopathy in dancers found mean return to dance of 16 weeks (range 8–36 weeks) following surgical decompression of the FHL sheath, with low complication rates. Conservative management (load modification, eccentric exercise, corticosteroid injection) was studied in very few cases, making evidence-based non-surgical protocols difficult to define — early referral to a clinician with dance medicine experience is recommended.[7]

6. Intrinsic Foot Musculature

The intrinsic foot muscles are organised into four plantar layers (numbered from superficial to deep) and a dorsal layer (extensor digitorum brevis / extensor hallucis brevis). They are responsible for fine control of MTP and IP joint movement, dynamic stabilisation of the metatarsophalangeal joints during late stance, and supplementary support of the longitudinal and transverse arches.

The nerve supply is almost entirely from the two terminal branches of the tibial nerve after it exits the tarsal tunnel: the medial plantar nerve (predominantly layer 1, medial muscles — analogous to the median nerve in the hand) and the lateral plantar nerve (the remaining deeper muscles — analogous to the ulnar nerve in the hand). The extensor digitorum brevis on the dorsum is the only intrinsic supplied by the deep fibular nerve.

Layer 1 — Superficial Plantar

The three muscles of the first plantar layer — abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis (FDB), and abductor digiti minimi — all originate from the calcaneal tuberosity and plantar aponeurosis. Abductor hallucis is the largest and most important arch supporter of the group; its hypertrophy (from barefoot strengthening programmes) has been shown to correlate with improved arch height and reduced plantar fascia load. FDB mirrors the superficial forearm flexors — its tendons split around the FDL tendons at the PIP joints.

Layer 2 — Quadratus Plantae and Lumbricals

Quadratus plantae (flexor accessorius) has a unique function: it re-aligns the oblique pull of FDL (which would otherwise pull the toes into an oblique rather than straight flexion) into a more sagittal plane. The four lumbricals of the foot, like those of the hand, flex the MTP joints while simultaneously extending the IP joints via the extensor hoods — a combination essential for maintaining toe alignment during push-off.

Layers 3 and 4 — Deep Intrinsics

Flexor hallucis brevis (FHB) is critical for protecting the sesamoid bones beneath the first metatarsal head — its two heads insert on either side of the proximal phalanx of the hallux via the sesamoids. Adductor hallucis (oblique and transverse heads) is the primary dynamic support of the transverse arch and actively opposes hallux valgus deformity. The dorsal interossei abduct and the plantar interossei adduct the toes relative to the 2nd toe axis (DAB/PAD — identical logic to the hand).

Evidence — Intrinsic foot strengthening: Multiple randomised controlled trials have demonstrated that a 4–8 week programme of short-foot exercise (a specific intrinsic activation technique targeting abductor hallucis) significantly increases abductor hallucis cross-sectional area and improves navicular drop values, suggesting a meaningful role for intrinsic strengthening in the management of pes planus and plantar fascia enthesopathy.[1]
