1. Overview of the Ankle & Foot Complex

The ankle and foot constitute one of the most mechanically demanding regions of the musculoskeletal system, required to absorb multiples of body weight at heel strike, adapt to irregular terrain during midstance, and generate propulsive forces at toe-off — all within a fraction of a second. These functions are accomplished through a hierarchy of joints working in coordinated sequence: the talocrural joint (true ankle), the subtalar joint, and the transverse tarsal (midtarsal) joint, supported by layered ligamentous structures on the lateral and medial aspects.

The complex can be divided into the rearfoot (calcaneus, talus), midfoot (navicular, cuboid, three cuneiforms), and forefoot (metatarsals, phalanges). Proximal control is transmitted from the tibia and fibula via the ankle mortise; distal stability depends on the intrinsic ligamentous architecture and the dynamic contribution of the long and short muscles crossing the ankle.

Functional note: In vivo kinematic studies demonstrate that talocrural dorsiflexion–plantarflexion and subtalar inversion–eversion are not purely uniplanar; both joints contribute to triplanar motion, and their coupling is essential for normal gait mechanics.[1]

2. Talocrural Joint — The Ankle Mortise

The talocrural joint is a synovial hinge joint formed by the distal tibia (plafond), the medial malleolus, the lateral malleolus of the fibula, and the superior dome (trochlea) of the talus. Together, the malleoli form the mortise that grips the talar trochlea. Because the trochlea is wider anteriorly than posteriorly, its shape has important kinematic consequences.

Arthrokinematics

During dorsiflexion the talus rolls anteriorly and simultaneously glides posteriorly within the mortise. The wider anterior talar dome is drawn into the mortise, spreading the malleoli and placing the ankle in its close-packed position — maximising bony congruency and joint stability. This explains why fractures during falls commonly involve the dorsiflexed ankle and why dorsiflexion restriction is clinically significant in gait assessment.[2]

During plantarflexion the narrower posterior trochlea occupies the mortise, reducing bony contact and increasing ligamentous dependence. This open-packed position renders the joint most vulnerable to sprain — the classic inversion sprain mechanism occurs in plantarflexion precisely because bony stability is lowest at this point.

Evidence: Human in vivo talocrural kinematic data during walking, running and hopping confirm that talocrural contributions are dominant in the sagittal plane, while subtalar joint contributions dominate frontal and transverse plane motion during dynamic loading.[1]

Capsule and Capsular Reinforcement

The talocrural joint capsule is thin anteriorly and posteriorly, reinforced laterally by the ATFL, CFL and PTFL, and medially by the deep layers of the deltoid ligament. The posterior capsule is thickened to form the posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL), which also contributes to syndesmotic integrity. The anterior capsule is frequently damaged in ankle sprains as the foot moves into plantarflexion and inversion.

3. Distal Tibiofibular Syndesmosis

The distal tibiofibular syndesmosis is a fibrous joint critical to the integrity of the ankle mortise. It is maintained by four structures: the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL), the posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL), the interosseous tibiofibular ligament, and the interosseous membrane extending proximally. Collectively these ligaments bind the fibula to the fibular notch (incisura fibularis) of the tibia.

The syndesmosis allows approximately 1–2 mm of fibular spreading and slight fibular rotation during full dorsiflexion, accommodating the wider anterior talar dome. Disruption of this arrangement — syndesmotic sprain or "high ankle sprain" — compromises the mortise width and threatens talocrural stability.

Mechanism of Injury

The dominant mechanism is forced external rotation of the talus with the foot in dorsiflexion. This drives the fibula posterolaterally and ruptures the AITFL first, followed by the interosseous ligament and PITFL if the force is severe. A high ankle sprain can occur in isolation or alongside a Maisonneuve fracture of the proximal fibula.[3]

Clinical note: Syndesmotic injuries are characterised by tenderness above the ankle joint line, a positive squeeze test and external rotation stress test. They carry a notably longer recovery time than lateral ligament sprains — typically 6–12 weeks compared to 2–6 weeks for isolated ATFL injuries. Management in athletes now frequently involves suture-button (endobutton) fixation, which allows some dynamic fibular motion and achieves outcomes comparable to screw fixation while avoiding obligate hardware removal.[4]
Evidence: A 2024 IJSPT clinical case series reported that an endobutton construct for isolated syndesmotic injuries achieved full return to sport in 95% of high-level athletes at a mean of 9.4 weeks, supporting dynamic fixation as the preferred approach over static screw fixation in active populations.[4]

4. Subtalar Joint & Transverse Tarsal Complex

The subtalar (talocalcaneal) joint lies immediately below the talocrural joint and is the primary articulation for inversion and eversion of the rearfoot. Its oblique joint axis (approximately 42° from horizontal, 23° from the sagittal plane) means that true subtalar motion is triplanar: supination (inversion + adduction + plantarflexion) and pronation (eversion + abduction + dorsiflexion).

During the loading response of gait, the subtalar joint pronates to unlock the midfoot for shock absorption and terrain adaptation. From midstance to push-off it supinates, stiffening the foot into a rigid lever for propulsion. This controlled transition is tightly coupled to tibial rotation: subtalar pronation drives internal tibial rotation, and supination drives external rotation — a relationship with important implications for knee and hip mechanics.

Sinus Tarsi Syndrome

The sinus tarsi is a lateral osteofibrous tunnel between the neck of the talus and the anterior calcaneus, containing the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament (ITCL), cervical ligament, fatty tissue, and a rich neurovascular supply. Sinus tarsi syndrome (STS) presents as persistent anterolateral ankle pain following lateral ankle sprain, attributed to ligamentous disruption of the ITCL and subsequent subtalar instability, synovitis, and scar tissue infiltration of the sinus.[5]

Evidence: Contemporary understanding positions STS primarily as a subtalar instability syndrome rather than a purely nociceptive condition; MRI-confirmed sinus tarsi lesions are strongly associated with prior inversion sprain, and corticosteroid injection combined with proprioceptive rehabilitation produces good short-term outcomes in the majority of cases.[5]

Transverse Tarsal (Chopart) Joint

The transverse tarsal joint comprises two articulations: the talonavicular joint (medially) and the calcaneocuboid joint (laterally). Collectively they allow the midfoot to twist relative to the rearfoot. A critical property of this joint complex is its axis-locking mechanism: when the subtalar joint is supinated (axes of talonavicular and calcaneocuboid converge), the Chopart joint becomes rigid; when the subtalar joint pronates (axes diverge), the midfoot unlocks. This elegant coupling provides stiffness for propulsion and flexibility for adaptation without requiring active muscle energy.

5. Lateral Ligament Complex

The lateral ankle is stabilised by three discrete ligaments: the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), and the posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL). These are anatomically and biomechanically distinct; they act sequentially depending on foot position and force direction.

Ligament Origin → Insertion Primary Role Position at Risk
ATFL Ant. fibula → ant-lat talus Limits ant. talar translation; limits internal rotation in plantarflexion Plantarflexion + inversion
CFL Tip of fibula → lat. calcaneus Limits inversion; stabilises subtalar joint; limits CFL-talocrural complex Neutral / dorsiflexion + inversion
PTFL Post. fibula → post-lat talus Limits posterior talar translation; strongest of the three Full dorsiflexion (rarely injured alone)

Lateral Ankle Sprain — Mechanism and Grading

Lateral ankle sprain is the most common musculoskeletal injury in sport, representing up to 25% of all sporting injuries. The classic mechanism is rapid plantarflexion combined with inversion and internal rotation, most commonly during landing. The ATFL is the first ligament to fail because it is the weakest and most taut in plantarflexion. Grade I sprains involve microscopic ATFL tears; Grade II add partial ATFL rupture; Grade III involve complete ATFL rupture, frequently with concurrent CFL rupture.[6]

Evidence (PMC10641652): A 2023 systematic review of conservative management confirmed that functional rehabilitation — early mobilisation, proprioceptive training, and graduated loading — is superior to immobilisation for Grade I–II lateral ankle sprains, producing faster return to activity and equivalent long-term stability. Functional treatment is now considered the gold standard across international guidelines.[6]

Chronic Lateral Ankle Instability (CLAI)

Approximately 20–40% of acute lateral ankle sprains progress to chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI), characterised by recurrent giving-way, persistent pain, and deficits in proprioception and neuromuscular control. CLAI is defined by functional instability (subjective) and/or mechanical instability (objective laxity on stress testing or imaging). Recent comparative trials have examined whether combined ATFL + CFL repair provides superior outcomes to ATFL repair alone in CLAI, with emerging evidence favouring combined repair in patients with significant hindfoot varus or CFL laxity on examination.[7]

Evidence (PMC12255937): A 2025 multicentre RCT found that combined ATFL/CFL repair corrected hindfoot varus alignment and improved AOFAS and FAAM scores at 24 months compared to ATFL repair alone in patients with concomitant hindfoot varus, suggesting the CFL is a key stabiliser of rearfoot alignment in CLAI.[7]

6. Deltoid (Medial) Ligament Complex

The deltoid ligament is a broad, fan-shaped structure on the medial aspect of the ankle that provides resistance to eversion, talar external rotation, and excessive abduction. Despite its strength — it is substantially stronger than the lateral ligament complex — it is injured less frequently, accounting for approximately 3–4% of isolated ankle ligament injuries. This reflects both its greater tensile strength and the relatively uncommon mechanism of pure eversion injury.

The deltoid complex is traditionally divided into superficial and deep layers. The superficial layer includes: the tibionavicular ligament (TNL), the tibiocalcaneal ligament (TCL), and the superficial posterior tibiotalar ligament (sPTTL). The deep layer comprises the deep anterior and deep posterior tibiotalar ligaments (dATTL and dPTTL). Recent anatomical studies have proposed a further division, recognising a "rear attachment of the deep deltoid" (RAD) as a distinct functional component contributing to talar rotational stability.[8, 9]

Evidence (PMC11605033): A 2024 KSSTA review found that isolated deltoid ligament injuries are frequently associated with rotational ankle instability rather than pure medial instability. The deep deltoid components — particularly the dPTTL — are the primary restraints against external talar rotation, and their disruption in the context of ankle fractures significantly increases tibiofibular diastasis and mortise widening.[8]
Clinical note: Medial ankle instability is often underdiagnosed. Eversion stress testing and anteromedial palpation (for the deep deltoid) are more informative than superficial palpation alone. Associated injuries — syndesmotic sprain, fibular fracture, osteochondral talus lesion — must be screened as the deltoid rarely fails in true isolation.

7. Plantar Fascia & Windlass Mechanism

The plantar fascia (plantar aponeurosis) is a dense, multilayered connective tissue structure originating from the medial calcaneal tubercle and inserting via five slips into the bases of the proximal phalanges. It is the primary passive support structure of the longitudinal arch of the foot, bearing significant tensile load during the stance phase of gait.

Windlass Mechanism

The windlass mechanism describes the passive stiffening of the foot that occurs when the toes are dorsiflexed. As the proximal phalanges extend (particularly the hallux), the plantar fascia winds around the metatarsal heads, shortening its effective length and drawing the calcaneus and metatarsals closer together — elevating the arch, supinating the subtalar joint, and externally rotating the tibia. This mechanism converts a flexible, shock-absorbing midstance foot into a rigid propulsive lever at push-off without requiring active muscular energy expenditure.[10]

Evidence (PMC385265): Biomechanical modelling demonstrates that high plantar fascia loads in the windlass mechanism are a significant etiological factor in plantar fasciitis, hallux valgus, and hallux limitus. Excessive subtalar pronation disrupts the efficiency of the windlass by preventing the supination required to stiffen the arch, placing abnormal tensile load on the fascial enthesis at the calcaneal tubercle.[10]

Plantar Fasciitis / Plantar Fascia Enthesopathy

Plantar fasciitis (more accurately termed plantar fascia enthesopathy in chronic presentations, reflecting the degenerative rather than inflammatory nature of the pathology) is the most common cause of inferior heel pain, affecting approximately 10% of people in their lifetime. The classical presentation is sharp, stabbing pain at the medial calcaneal tubercle on first steps in the morning, easing with activity but worsening after prolonged standing.

Evidence: High-quality evidence supports load management, calf and plantar fascia stretching, and progressive loading (including high-load strength training via heavy slow resistance) as first-line management. Shockwave therapy demonstrates moderate evidence for chronic cases. Corticosteroid injection provides short-term pain relief but does not address the underlying enthesopathy and carries a risk of fascial rupture with repeated use. Most cases resolve within 6–12 months with conservative management.
