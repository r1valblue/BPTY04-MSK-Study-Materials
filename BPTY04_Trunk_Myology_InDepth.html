Trunk Myology — In-Depth Study Guide

BPTY04 Musculoskeletal Anatomy · LUNEX University · Evidence-based review with clinical applications

1. Overview: The Trunk as a Functional System

The muscles of the trunk do far more than simply move the axial skeleton — they form an integrated mechanical system that supports posture, transfers load between the upper and lower limbs, protects the spinal cord, controls intra-abdominal pressure, and coordinates breathing with movement. Understanding trunk myology therefore requires moving beyond individual muscle memorisation toward a systems-level perspective.

Anatomically, the trunk musculature spans a large territory: from the suboccipital muscles at the craniovertebral junction down through the muscles of mastication, the deep cervical flexors, the thoracic cage musculature, the erector spinae and deep intrinsic back muscles, the abdominal wall, and ultimately the diaphragm and pelvic floor. Each region has distinct biomechanical roles, but all are functionally interdependent.

This document organises trunk myology into five major functional themes: (1) craniovertebral and cervical control, (2) the erector spinae and deep intrinsic back muscles, (3) the thoracic cage and breathing musculature, (4) the abdominal wall and spinal stabilisation, and (5) the diaphragm as a multi-role organ.

2. Craniovertebral and Cervical Control

2.1 Suboccipital Muscles and the Suboccipital Triangle

The four pairs of suboccipital muscles — rectus capitis posterior major and minor, obliquus capitis superior and inferior — are the smallest and deepest muscles acting on the craniovertebral region. Despite their small size, they are densely packed with proprioceptive muscle spindles, making them key sensory transducers for head position rather than primary force generators.

The obliquus capitis inferior, obliquus capitis superior, and rectus capitis posterior major form the suboccipital triangle, a space through which the vertebral artery and the first cervical posterior ramus (suboccipital nerve) travel. Tightness of these muscles — common in people with sustained flexed-neck postures and screen-based work — can irritate the greater occipital nerve (C2) causing suboccipital headache, a pattern that is sometimes confused with tension-type or migraine headache.

Evidence Note The suboccipital muscles have direct myodural connections to the spinal dura mater. A 2020 systematic review (PMC7425638) documented these bridging connective tissue structures and proposed that suboccipital muscle dysfunction may transmit tension directly to the dura, contributing to headache. Suboccipital release techniques in physiotherapy aim to normalise this tension.

2.2 Deep Cervical Flexors: Longus Colli and Longus Capitis

The deep cervical flexors — primarily longus colli (pars recta, pars obliqua superior, pars obliqua inferior) and longus capitis — provide segmental control of the cervical lordosis. Unlike the superficial sternocleidomastoid which flexes the entire cervical column, the deep cervical flexors produce the craniocervical nodding movement (upper cervical flexion) and offer precise intersegmental control.

In people with chronic neck pain and cervicogenic headache, deep cervical flexor strength and endurance are consistently reduced. The Cranio-Cervical Flexion Test (CCFT) assesses deep cervical flexor activation via pressure biofeedback at the suboccipital region and is a standard clinical assessment tool in cervical spine physiotherapy.

2.3 Scalene Muscles and Thoracic Outlet Syndrome

The three scalene muscles (anterior, medius, posterior) run from the transverse processes of C3–C7 to the first and second ribs, forming the lateral wall of the scalene triangle. The brachial plexus (specifically the C5–T1 nerve roots) passes between the anterior and middle scalenes, while the subclavian artery runs immediately anterior to the brachial plexus and posterior to the anterior scalene. The subclavian vein passes anterior to the anterior scalene, outside this triangle.

Hypertrophy, spasm, or anatomical variation (such as a cervical rib or anomalous first rib) can reduce the available space in the scalene triangle and compress these neurovascular structures — the mechanism underlying thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS). Neurogenic TOS (compression of the brachial plexus) is by far the most common variety, presenting with upper limb paraesthesia and weakness in the C8–T1 distribution. Physiotherapy management includes scalene stretching, thoracic mobility work, and posture correction.

Evidence Note StatPearls (NBK519058) and a 2021 PMC review (PMC8480370) confirm the variability in scalene anatomy — including accessory scalene muscles and variable brachial plexus root passages — which contributes to the variable clinical presentation of TOS. Up to 10% of the population may have a cervical rib, though most are asymptomatic.

2.4 Sternocleidomastoid (SCM)

The SCM is the largest and most clinically visible cervical muscle. It arises via two heads — sternal (from the anterior surface of the manubrium) and clavicular (from the medial third of the clavicle) — and inserts on the mastoid process and lateral part of the superior nuchal line. Bilateral contraction produces cervical flexion; unilateral contraction produces ipsilateral lateral flexion combined with contralateral rotation. SCM is innervated by CN XI (spinal accessory nerve) and C2–C3 sensory fibres.

Clinically, SCM involvement appears in several contexts. Torticollis (wry neck) involves unilateral SCM spasm or congenital shortening. Accessory nerve damage (e.g., following cervical lymph node dissection in head and neck cancer surgery) leads to SCM weakness and shoulder drop (due to concurrent trapezius involvement). In acute locked neck (facet joint "locking"), SCM often goes into protective spasm maintaining the head in the direction of comfort.

🏥 Clinical: Cervicogenic Headache Muscle Profile Patients with cervicogenic headache typically present with: increased tone in suboccipital muscles, upper trapezius, and sternocleidomastoid; reduced deep cervical flexor endurance (CCFT); restricted C0–C1 and C1–C2 joint mobility; and tenderness of the greater occipital nerve at the C2 level. A 2024 RCT (PMC11205762) found that cervical mobilisation combined with clinical Pilates reduced headache frequency and decreased suboccipital, upper trapezius, and SCM stiffness.

3. Erector Spinae and Deep Intrinsic Back Muscles

3.1 Erector Spinae: Architecture and Function

The erector spinae group forms the dominant posterior spinal extensor system. From lateral to medial, its three sub-groups are iliocostalis, longissimus, and spinalis. All share a common origin via the thoracolumbar aponeurosis from the posterior sacrum, iliac crest, and lumbar and lower thoracic spinous processes, then diverge superiorly to insert on posterior ribs, spinous and transverse processes, and (in the case of longissimus capitis) the mastoid process.

Bilaterally, the erector spinae extend the vertebral column and resist forward trunk flexion (eccentric control during forward bending is a key functional role). Unilaterally, they produce ipsilateral lateral flexion. During heavy lifting, they act in concert with the thoracolumbar fascia to create a posterior tension band that offloads the intervertebral discs.

3.2 Multifidus: The Segmental Stabiliser

The multifidus runs from the sacrum, ilium, and lumbar mamillary processes to insert 2–4 vertebral levels higher on spinous processes. Unlike the erector spinae — which span many segments — the multifidus has a segmental, one-to-one stabilising relationship with individual motion segments. Its deep fibres are particularly important for controlling intersegmental movement (translation and rotation of individual vertebrae), while its superficial fibres contribute more to gross extension torque.

The multifidus is a key target in low back pain rehabilitation. Its importance rests on three lines of evidence: first, it atrophies rapidly and asymmetrically after acute lumbar disc injury; second, this atrophy does not spontaneously recover even after pain resolves; and third, specific multifidus training (segmental stabilisation exercises) has been shown to reduce LBP recurrence.

Evidence Note — Multifidus Atrophy A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the European Spine Journal (Springer, doi:10.1007/s00586-025-09454-z) confirmed that paraspinal muscles — multifidus in particular — show significantly greater fat infiltration and reduced cross-sectional area in chronic LBP patients compared to controls. A parallel 2025 review in the International Journal of Spine Surgery (IJSS 8814) found that restorative neurostimulation of the multifidus, alongside motor control exercises, offered a rehabilitative rather than purely palliative treatment approach. A 2025 review in Pain Practice (doi:10.1111/papr.70044) further summarised the evidence for motor control exercise and multifidus-targeted interventions in clinical management.

3.3 Semispinalis, Rotatores, and the Transversospinal System

The transversospinal muscles — rotatores, multifidus, and semispinalis — all run from transverse processes to spinous processes of higher vertebrae, spanning 1–2 levels (rotatores), 2–4 levels (multifidus), and 4–6 levels (semispinalis) respectively. Together they form a graduated force-coupling system for spinal extension and contralateral rotation. The semispinalis capitis, with its broad origin from C3–T6 transverse processes and insertion on the occipital bone, is one of the primary extensors of the head and upper cervical spine.

3.4 Minor Intrinsic Back Muscles

The intertransversarii and interspinales are small muscles connecting adjacent vertebrae. While too small to generate significant torque, they contain a high density of muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs, making them primarily proprioceptive organs. The levatores costarum (C7–T11 to the ribs below) contribute to both thoracic extension and rib elevation during deep inspiration. The splenius cervicis and capitis, lying more superficially over the semispinalis, act as unilateral head rotators and extensors and are commonly taut in patients with forward head posture.

🏥 Clinical: Lumbar Stabilisation Programme Rationale The clinical rationale for core stabilisation programmes rests on the "inner core" model: multifidus, transversus abdominis, diaphragm, and pelvic floor together create a cylinder of pressure and stiffness around the lumbar spine. After an episode of LBP, the inner core undergoes motor control changes (delayed onset, reduced tonic activity) that are not corrected by pain resolution alone. Stage 1 of a stabilisation programme specifically targets isolated multifidus and TrA co-contraction before progressing to loaded functional tasks.

4. Thoracic Cage and Breathing Musculature

4.1 Intercostal Muscles

Eleven external intercostal muscles and eleven internal intercostal muscles fill the intercostal spaces. The external intercostals run from the costal tubercle (posterior) to the chondro-osseous junction (anterior) in a ventrocaudal direction (like fingers in a pocket going down-forward). Their geometry means that contraction raises the ribs, expanding the thoracic cavity — making them inspiratory muscles. The internal intercostals run dorsocaudally (crossed-arm direction) and lower the ribs, reducing thoracic volume — an expiratory function.

Both sets are innervated by the corresponding intercostal nerves (Nn. intercostales 1–11), which run in the costal groove along the inferior border of each rib in a neurovascular bundle (vein, artery, nerve — VAN from superior to inferior). This anatomical relationship is important during thoracentesis (pleural tap), which is performed just above the superior border of a rib to avoid this bundle.

4.2 Transversus Thoracis and Subcostales

The transversus thoracis (also called the sternocostalis) originates from the inner surface of the sternum and xiphoid and radiates to the costal cartilages of ribs 2–6. The subcostales are thin slips on the inner surface of the posterior thoracic wall that bridge across one or two ribs. Both are active in forced expiration, helping to lower the rib cage. Their clinical relevance is limited but they form part of the thoracic cage anatomy assessed in cadaveric dissection.

4.3 Serratus Posterior Muscles

The serratus posterior superior (nuchal ligament and SP C7–T3 → superior border of ribs 2–5) acts as an accessory inspiratory muscle. The serratus posterior inferior (SP T11–L2 → inferior border of ribs 9–11) anchors the lower ribs against the pull of the diaphragm during deep inspiration, preventing the lower thoracic aperture from narrowing. Both are innervated by the anterior branches of the intercostal nerves and are clinically noteworthy primarily as sources of posterior thoracic pain and as muscles addressed in breathing rehabilitation.

🏥 Clinical: Rib Fracture and Paradoxical Breathing Normally the chest wall moves outward on inspiration as the intercostals raise the ribs. In a flail chest (multiple rib fractures creating a free-floating segment), that segment moves paradoxically inward on inspiration due to negative intrathoracic pressure. This impairs ventilation and is a clinical emergency. Understanding intercostal anatomy and rib mechanics is directly relevant to trauma assessment.

5. Abdominal Wall and Spinal Stabilisation

5.1 Layered Architecture of the Abdominal Wall

The four abdominal wall muscles — obliquus externus abdominis, obliquus internus abdominis, transversus abdominis, and rectus abdominis — are arranged concentrically from superficial to deep. All four contribute to trunk flexion, compression of abdominal contents, and expiratory effort, but each has distinct spinal stabilisation and rotation roles.

The obliquus externus runs ventrocaudally from the outer surface of ribs 5–12 to the iliac crest and linea alba. Its unilateral action is ipsilateral lateral flexion and contralateral rotation — the fibres are oriented like the hands in pockets direction, pulling the contralateral pelvis toward the ribs. The obliquus internus runs caudoventrally from the thoracolumbar fascia and iliac crest to the lower ribs and linea alba; its unilateral action is ipsilateral inclination and ipsilateral rotation. For any given direction of trunk rotation, the ipsilateral obliquus internus and the contralateral obliquus externus act as an oblique force couple.

5.2 Transversus Abdominis and Intra-Abdominal Pressure

The transversus abdominis (TrA) is the deepest of the four abdominal muscles. Running horizontally from the inner surface of the lower costal cartilages, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, and inguinal ligament to the linea alba and rectus sheath, it wraps around the trunk like a corset. Rather than producing trunk rotation or flexion, TrA's primary role is to increase intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) and tension the thoracolumbar fascia, thereby increasing lumbar spinal stiffness.

A landmark finding by Hodges and Richardson (1996, Spine) showed that TrA activates prior to voluntary limb movements in healthy subjects — a feedforward postural response — but this anticipatory activation is delayed or absent in people with chronic low back pain. This finding provided the scientific rationale for specific TrA training ("drawing-in" manoeuvre) in LBP rehabilitation.

Evidence Note — TrA and Spinal Stability Research reviewed by esmed.org (Role of transversus abdominis in spinal stability) demonstrated that spinal stability increased by an average factor of 1.8 with a doubling of intra-abdominal pressure. A PMC review (PMC3157598) found that abdominal co-contraction significantly increased lumbar spinal stability. Critically, TrA contributes to stability largely independent of the direction of trunk movement — unlike the other abdominals whose stabilising role varies with direction — making it uniquely suited as a tonic "stiffener" rather than a phasic mover.

5.3 Rectus Abdominis and Quadratus Lumborum

The rectus abdominis lies within the rectus sheath, running vertically from the costal cartilages of ribs 5–7 and xiphoid process to the pubic crest. It is the prime mover for trunk flexion (crunches, sit-ups) and for forced expiration. The three tendinous intersections visible in a developed athlete divide the muscle into segments and are fused to the anterior rectus sheath, preventing the muscle from bowing outward under load.

The quadratus lumborum (QL) connects the iliac crest to the 12th rib and lumbar transverse processes. Bilaterally it extends the lumbar spine and fixes the 12th rib during deep inspiration (preventing the lower thoracic aperture from collapsing). Unilaterally it produces ipsilateral lateral flexion and is a common source of acute low back pain and lateral hip pain. The QL is often inappropriately tight in people with leg length discrepancy or hip abductor weakness.

🏥 Clinical: Diastasis Rectus Abdominis (DRA) During pregnancy, the linea alba — the fibrous raphe between the two rectus abdominis muscles — can widen (normally <2 cm) due to the expanding uterus, hormonal laxity, and increased IAP. Postpartum DRA can reduce abdominal wall stiffness and contribute to pelvic girdle pain and LBP. Rehabilitation focuses on restoring linea alba tension via deep abdominal activation (TrA) and avoiding exercises that generate high IAP or excessive doming of the linea alba.

6. The Diaphragm: Respiration, Pressure, and Stability

6.1 Anatomy

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped musculotendinous partition separating the thorax from the abdomen. It consists of a central tendon (the aponeurotic insertion for all muscular fibres) and three peripheral muscular parts: the sternal part (from the posterior surface of the xiphoid process), the costal part (from the inner surface of the lower six ribs and their cartilages, interdigitating with transversus abdominis), and the lumbar part.

The lumbar part arises via two crura and three pairs of arcuate ligaments. The medial and lateral arcuate ligaments arch over the psoas major and quadratus lumborum respectively. The right and left crura arise from the vertebral bodies of L1–L3 (right, longer) and L1–L2 (left) and blend with the anterior longitudinal ligament. The right crus forms a muscular hiatus through which the oesophagus passes (the oesophageal hiatus at T10, a site susceptible to hiatal hernias).

Three major openings transmit structures: the vena cava hiatus (T8, in the central tendon), the oesophageal hiatus (T10, in the right crus), and the aortic hiatus (T12, between the crura and vertebral column — transmitting the aorta, thoracic duct, and azygos vein). The mnemonic "I 8 (ate) Ten Eggs At 12" captures levels T8, T10, T12.

6.2 Innervation: The Phrenic Nerve

The diaphragm is innervated exclusively by the phrenic nerve (from C3, C4, and C5 — "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive"). The phrenic nerves descend through the thorax, passing anterior to the lung root on each side, to supply the diaphragm from below. Referred pain from diaphragmatic irritation (e.g., subphrenic abscess, pneumonia affecting the diaphragmatic pleura) is experienced in the shoulder tip (C4 dermatome) due to convergence of somatic and visceral afferents at the C4 level.

6.3 Respiratory Function

During quiet inspiration, diaphragmatic contraction flattens the dome, increasing vertical thoracic diameter and generating negative intrathoracic pressure. This is the primary mechanism of breathing at rest, with the external intercostals contributing secondarily to rib cage expansion. The accessory muscles of inspiration (scalenes, sternocleidomastoid, pectoralis minor, serratus anterior) are recruited during exercise or in respiratory distress to further expand the thoracic cage.

During forced expiration — coughing, sneezing, Valsalva — the diaphragm relaxes and the internal intercostals, transversus thoracis, subcostales, and abdominal wall muscles contract forcefully. This produces a sharp rise in IAP that drives air out of the lungs and also transmits a large compressive load to the lumbar spine, which must be stabilised by the intrinsic back muscles and abdominal wall simultaneously.

6.4 The Diaphragm as a Postural Muscle

Beyond respiration, the diaphragm has a critical but often overlooked role in spinal stabilisation. When a person braces for a lifting task, the diaphragm, pelvic floor, TrA, and multifidus co-contract simultaneously to create a pressurised cylinder of support around the lumbar spine. The diaphragm forms the roof of this cylinder, the pelvic floor the floor, and TrA and multifidus the walls.

Diaphragm dysfunction — whether from pain inhibition, chronic upper-chest breathing patterns, or respiratory conditions — can therefore impair lumbar stability even in the absence of direct spinal pathology. People who habitually use the upper thoracic accessory muscles rather than diaphragmatic breathing show reduced IAP responses and impaired lumbar spinal stiffness during movement.

Evidence Note — Diaphragm and Low Back Pain A narrative review (JOSPT 2012) found that chronic LBP patients showed significantly reduced postural function of the diaphragm during unilateral leg loading tasks. A 2024 RCT (PMC10774616) demonstrated that adding diaphragmatic breathing exercises to core stabilisation exercises produced greater reductions in pain, disability, and improved sleep quality compared to core stabilisation alone in chronic LBP patients. A narrative review in JBMT (PubMed 37949564) recommended initiating core stabilisation programmes with facilitation of normal breathing patterns before progressing to loaded spinal exercises.
🏥 Clinical: Phrenic Nerve Injury and Spinal Cord Injury Spinal cord injury at or above C3 causes complete diaphragmatic paralysis and requires permanent ventilatory support. Injury between C3 and C5 may partially spare phrenic function, with some patients achieving partial ventilator independence over time due to diaphragmatic sparing by the level of injury. Injury below C5 leaves the diaphragm intact but paralyses the intercostals and abdominals, reducing expiratory force (affected patients cannot cough effectively, putting them at high risk of respiratory infection). This graded dependency on spinal level is a central concept in spinal cord injury rehabilitation.
