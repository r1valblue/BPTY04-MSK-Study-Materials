Recall Questions
Recall
1. Name the four muscles of the anterior compartment of the leg and their single shared nerve supply.
Muscles: (1) Tibialis anterior, (2) Extensor hallucis longus (EHL), (3) Extensor digitorum longus (EDL), (4) Fibularis (peroneus) tertius.

Nerve: Deep fibular (peroneal) nerve — L4–L5. All four share this innervation.
Recall
2. Describe the origin and insertion of tibialis anterior and list its two actions.
Origin: Lateral tibial condyle, upper 2/3 of lateral tibial shaft, interosseous membrane.
Insertion: Plantar surface of medial cuneiform and base of 1st metatarsal.
Actions: (1) Dorsiflexion of the ankle; (2) Inversion (supination) of the foot.
Recall
3. Name the two lateral compartment muscles, their nerve supply, and their primary action.
Muscles: Fibularis (peroneus) longus and fibularis (peroneus) brevis.
Nerve: Superficial fibular (peroneal) nerve — L5–S1.
Primary action: Eversion of the foot. Both also contribute to plantarflexion.
Recall
4. What are the Triceps Surae? Name the three muscles and state the key functional difference between gastrocnemius and soleus.
Triceps Surae: Gastrocnemius + soleus + plantaris. All insert via the Achilles tendon; all supplied by tibial nerve S1–S2.

Key difference: Gastrocnemius is biarticular (crosses knee and ankle) → most effective with knee extended. Soleus is monoarticular (ankle only) → effective regardless of knee position. Soleus is predominantly slow-twitch (postural); gastrocnemius has more fast-twitch fibres (propulsive).
Recall
5. Name the four muscles of the deep posterior compartment and their common nerve supply.
Muscles: (1) Popliteus, (2) Tibialis posterior, (3) Flexor digitorum longus (FDL), (4) Flexor hallucis longus (FHL).

Nerve: Tibial nerve — L4–S2 (specific roots vary per muscle).
Recall
6. Give the tarsal tunnel contents using the mnemonic "Tom Dick And a Nervous Harry." List the structures in order.
Tom = Tibialis Posterior
Dick = Flexor Digitorum Longus (FDL)
And a = posterior tibial Artery and veins
Nervous = tibial Nerve
Harry = Flexor Hallucis Longus (FHL)

These structures pass posterior to the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum (through the tarsal tunnel), from anterior to posterior.
Recall
7. Describe the primary insertion of tibialis posterior and explain why it is so broad.
Primary insertion: Navicular tuberosity (the main attachment).
Additional slips to: All three cuneiforms, cuboid, sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus, and the 2nd–4th metatarsal bases.

Why broad: Because tibialis posterior acts as a "suspension cable" for the entire medial longitudinal arch — broad attachment distributes the arch-supporting force across multiple midfoot bones, making it the key dynamic invertor and arch support.
Recall
8. Name the three plantar intrinsic muscles of Layer 1 (superficial plantar layer) and which nerve innervates each.
1. Abductor hallucis — Medial plantar nerve
2. Flexor digitorum brevis (FDB) — Medial plantar nerve
3. Abductor digiti minimi — Lateral plantar nerve

All originate from the calcaneal tuberosity / plantar aponeurosis.
Application Questions
Application
9. Why is tibialis anterior the primary muscle tested when screening for common fibular nerve palsy? Describe the resulting gait pattern if it fails.
Why tibialis anterior: It is the strongest and most clinically testable muscle of the deep fibular nerve territory (L4–L5). Loss of dorsiflexion is its hallmark deficit. It is the primary swing-phase dorsiflexor allowing toe clearance.

Resulting gait (foot drop): During swing phase, the foot cannot clear the ground → patient compensates with steppage gait (exaggerated hip and knee flexion) and/or circumduction of the affected limb. On landing, the foot "slaps" down (flaccid anterior compartment).
Application
10. Explain the unique course of fibularis longus and why it can support both the transverse and lateral arches despite arising from the fibula.
Fibularis longus passes posterior to the lateral malleolus, then turns anteriorly and crosses under the cuboid through the fibular groove (plantar aspect). It then crosses the plantar surface of the foot obliquely from lateral to medial, inserting on the plantar surface of the medial cuneiform and base of the 1st metatarsal.

This oblique plantar course creates a "stirrup" effect: combined with tibialis anterior (which also inserts on the medial cuneiform + 1st metatarsal, from the dorsal/medial side), they bind the medial and lateral foot together and bow the transverse arch. The lateral compartment position of the origin provides the eversion component.
Application
11. A runner can dorsiflex normally but cannot evert the foot against resistance. Which nerve and compartment are affected?
Nerve: Superficial fibular (peroneal) nerve — L5–S1.
Compartment: Lateral compartment (fibularis longus and fibularis brevis).

Key differentiation: Dorsiflexion is intact (deep fibular nerve, anterior compartment unaffected), but eversion is lost. This pattern isolates the superficial fibular nerve. Common causes: ankle sprain with nerve stretch, fibular head fracture, compartment syndrome of the lateral compartment, or entrapment at the fibular tunnel.
Application
12. Why is soleus called the "postural muscle" of plantarflexion while gastrocnemius is described as the "propulsive muscle"? Explain using fibre type and biarticulation.
Soleus — postural:
• Monoarticular → unaffected by knee position
• Predominantly slow-twitch (Type I) fibres → fatigue-resistant, suited to continuous low-level contraction
• Active during quiet standing to oppose anterior gravitational topple (prevents forward tibial drift)

Gastrocnemius — propulsive:
• Biarticular (knee + ankle) → contributes flexion torque at both joints simultaneously
• Higher proportion of fast-twitch (Type II) fibres → generates high force quickly
• Most active during push-off in running and jumping when the knee is near extension
• Less effective with a flexed knee (shortened proximally → active insufficiency)
Application
13. Describe the role of quadratus plantae and explain why FDL requires it to produce effective toe flexion.
FDL originates from the posterior tibia and its pull line runs obliquely — if uncorrected, it would flex the toes into an oblique diagonal direction rather than straight sagittal flexion.

Quadratus plantae (layer 2) arises from two heads on the medial and lateral calcaneus and inserts into the posterolateral aspect of the FDL tendon. Its contraction redirects the FDL pull into a more straight plantar direction, producing effective sagittal toe flexion.

This is analogous to the lumbrical origin from FDL tendons — both quadratus plantae and lumbricals modify the FDL's mechanical line of action.
Application
14. Apply the DAB/PAD rule to the foot interossei. Name the axis of abduction/adduction and compare to the hand.
DAB: Dorsal interossei Abduct (spread toes apart)
PAD: Plantar interossei Adduct (bring toes together)

Axis: The 2nd toe is the reference axis in the foot (all abduction/adduction is relative to the line of the 2nd toe). This differs from the hand, where the middle finger (3rd digit) is the reference axis.

There are 4 dorsal interossei (abductors of toes 2–4) and 3 plantar interossei (adductors of toes 3–5). All are supplied by the lateral plantar nerve. Both also flex the MTP joints and extend the IP joints via their extensor hood insertions.
Application
15. Explain why the Achilles hypovascular zone is at highest risk for both tendinopathy and rupture.
The Achilles tendon receives its blood supply from three sources: musculotendinous junction (proximally), the paratenon (along its length), and the calcaneal insertion (distally). The zone approximately 2–6 cm above the calcaneal insertion is a relative "watershed" area — where the terminal branches of all three sources are poorest, creating a zone of relative hypovascularity.

In this zone, repetitive mechanical stress (tendinopathy) or acute overload (rupture) outpaces the reduced healing capacity of the hypovascular tissue. Collagen disarray develops (tendinosis), weakening the tendon matrix until an eccentric force (e.g., sudden push-off) causes rupture. This also explains why intratendinous corticosteroid injections in this zone carry a higher rupture risk — they further impair collagen synthesis.
Application
16. What is the "knot of Henry" (master knot) and what structures are involved?
The knot of Henry (master knot of Henry) is the anatomical crossing point on the plantar foot where FHL and FDL cross each other and are connected by a fibrous slip.

It is located beneath the navicular / medial to the sustentaculum tali. At this point FHL is superficial (plantar) to FDL. A fibrous interconnection between the two tendons allows FHL to assist FDL flexion of the lateral toes. This crossing is clinically relevant in triggering: if FHL is thickened (from FHL tenosynovitis), it can snag at this crossing and cause a "trigger toe" — the hallux suddenly locks in flexion.
Clinical Questions
Clinical
17. A 55-year-old woman presents with medial ankle pain, progressive flatfoot, and inability to perform a single-leg heel raise. What is your working diagnosis, and outline your assessment and management?
Working diagnosis: Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (PTTD) / Progressive Collapsing Foot Deformity (PCFD) — Stage II.

Assessment:
• "Too many toes" sign (lateral toes visible from behind due to forefoot abduction)
• Single-leg heel raise test: failure = tibialis posterior insufficiency
• Navicular drop test: significant arch collapse
• Palpation along tibialis posterior tendon (posterior to medial malleolus → navicular): swelling, thickening, tenderness
• MRI to confirm tendon integrity and grade deformity

Management (Stage II — flexible deformity):
• UCBL orthosis or custom arch support to offload the failing tendon
• Eccentric tibialis posterior strengthening (single-leg heel raises on a decline board)
• NSAIDs for acute pain
• If conservative fails → surgical: tibialis posterior debridement + FDL transfer + calcaneal osteotomy
Clinical
18. A recreational runner presents with midportion Achilles pain 3 cm above the heel, worse at the start of a run then easing. Outline evidence-based management.
Diagnosis: Midportion Achilles tendinopathy (hypovascular zone, degenerative tendinosis).

Evidence-based management (2024 JOSPT CPG):
Phase 1 — Isometric loading: Sustained isometric heel raises (e.g., 5 × 45 s, twice daily) — immediate pain relief, tendon loading without provocative movement.
Phase 2 — Heavy slow resistance: Single-leg heel raises with progressive load (knee straight for gastrocnemius, knee bent for soleus isolation), 3 × 15 reps → 3 × 6 heavy, 3×/week over 12 weeks. Target: highest load tolerable (per 2024 CPG).
Phase 3 — Energy storage: Plyometric and running-specific loading once pain-free under heavy load.

Adjuncts: Load management (reduce running volume ~20%, no complete rest); shockwave therapy if no improvement at 3 months; avoid intratendinous corticosteroid injection (rupture risk).
Prognosis: Good — most resolve with 12 weeks of loading; recurrence risk reduced by ongoing maintenance exercise.
Clinical
19. A ballet dancer presents with posterior ankle pain during pointe work. What structure is most likely involved and how do you assess it?
Most likely structure: Flexor Hallucis Longus (FHL) tendon and/or posterior ankle impingement syndrome (PAIS), often involving the os trigonum.

Assessment:
• Palpation of FHL sheath posterior to the medial malleolus and between the two malleoli in the posterior ankle
• Thomasen's test: Passively flex/extend the hallux with the ankle in plantarflexion — reproduction of posterior ankle pain = positive for FHL tenosynovitis
• FHL triggering: palpable "clunk" during hallux IP joint flexion/extension = FHL nodule catching in sheath
• Pain with passive forced plantarflexion = posterior impingement (PAIS)
• MRI or ultrasound to assess tendon morphology and os trigonum presence

Management: Activity modification, anti-inflammatory measures, physiotherapy (calf flexibility, ankle mobility). If conservative treatment fails: FHL sheath surgical decompression ± os trigonum excision (mean return to dance 16 weeks post-op).
Clinical
20. During a lateral ankle inversion sprain assessment you notice a "clunk" on the lateral ankle during eversion. What additional diagnosis should you suspect and how do you confirm it?
Suspected diagnosis: Peroneal (fibularis) tendon subluxation — the tendons snap anteriorly over the lateral malleolus when the superior fibular retinaculum is disrupted.

Why missed: Up to 78% of peroneal subluxations co-occur with lateral ligament sprains, leading to frequent misdiagnosis. The clunk or snapping sensation is the key distinguishing feature not present in simple ATFL/CFL sprain.

Confirmation:
• Dynamic ultrasound: most sensitive — visualises real-time tendon subluxation with active dorsiflexion/eversion
• MRI: shows retinacular tear, peroneus brevis split tears, fluid in tendon sheath
• Provocative test: active dorsiflexion/eversion against resistance reproduces subluxation

Management: Acute — non-weight-bearing in eversion splint/cast 6 weeks; chronic — surgical retinaculum repair or fibular groove deepening procedure.
Clinical
21. Explain the clinical rationale for differentiating an eccentric calf raise with the knee straight vs. knee bent in Achilles tendinopathy rehabilitation.
Knee straight: Gastrocnemius is at near-optimal length → the eccentric load is directed primarily through the gastrocnemius musculotendinous unit → loads the proximal and mid-Achilles (relevant for midportion tendinopathy). This was the basis of the original Alfredson protocol.

Knee bent (approximately 45°): Gastrocnemius is shortened proximally (biarticular active insufficiency) → significantly reduces gastrocnemius contribution → the eccentric load is shifted predominantly to the soleus musculotendinous unit → loads the distal Achilles (relevant for insertional tendinopathy and soleus-dominant presentations).

Clinical implication: Both positions should be included in a complete rehab programme. The Alfredson protocol (3 × 15 each position, twice daily) uses both; modern high-load slow resistance protocols reduce repetitions but increase load. Knee-bent loading is particularly important for heavy athletes where soleus weakness is disproportionate.
Clinical
22. A patient reports burning, tingling, and numbness along the sole of the foot after a twisting ankle injury. Examination shows no motor deficit but positive Tinel's sign posterior to the medial malleolus. What is the diagnosis and what are possible causes?
Diagnosis: Tarsal tunnel syndrome — compression of the tibial nerve (or its branches) posterior to the medial malleolus within the tarsal tunnel (under the flexor retinaculum).

Clinical findings:
• Positive Tinel's sign (tapping over the tarsal tunnel reproduces distal tingling = nerve irritability)
• Burning/tingling/numbness in the plantar foot (tibial nerve distribution)
• No motor deficit if mild/early

Possible causes post-injury:
• Post-traumatic adhesions or tenosynovitis within the tunnel
• Haematoma from inversion sprain compressing the tunnel
• Space-occupying lesion (ganglion, varicosity)
• Excessive subtalar pronation (dynamic narrowing of the tunnel)

Management: Arch-supporting orthotics (reduces pronation-related narrowing); nerve gliding exercises; corticosteroid injection; surgical decompression for recalcitrant cases.

Clinical
24. A patient with progressive hallux valgus asks why it is getting worse. Explain the muscular imbalance involved and what intrinsic muscle is particularly relevant.
Muscular imbalance in hallux valgus:
As the hallux drifts into valgus (laterally), the sesamoids sublux laterally beneath the 1st metatarsal head. The adductor hallucis (already pulling the hallux laterally) gains mechanical advantage and continues to pull the hallux further into valgus. The abductor hallucis (normally pulling medially to resist valgus) migrates inferiorly and its pull line becomes less effective at correcting the deformity.

Key intrinsic muscle — Adductor hallucis: Its oblique and transverse heads normally provide the transverse arch support. In hallux valgus, the transverse head's attachment to the deep transverse metatarsal ligaments maintains a lateral pull on the hallux, progressively worsening the deformity in a positive feedback loop.

Clinical relevance: Strengthening abductor hallucis (short-foot exercises, toe spreading) can slow early progression. Advanced deformity requires osteotomy (e.g., Scarf or Austin/chevron) to realign the metatarsophalangeal joint mechanically.
