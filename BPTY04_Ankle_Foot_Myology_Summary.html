1. Overview & Movements (Slides 1–3)

Leg & Foot Compartments — at a glance

The muscles acting on the ankle and foot are grouped into four compartments of the leg plus the intrinsic foot muscles:
  • Anterior compartment: Dorsiflexion & toe extension — Deep fibular nerve (L4–L5)
  • Lateral compartment: Eversion — Superficial fibular nerve (L5–S1)
  • Superficial posterior: Plantarflexion (Triceps Surae) — Tibial nerve (S1–S2)
  • Deep posterior: Plantarflexion, inversion & toe flexion — Tibial nerve (L4–S2)
  • Intrinsic foot: Fine toe control & arch support — Plantar nerves (branches of tibial)

Anterior Foot Tendons — Slide 7

The anterior compartment tendons pass under the superior extensor retinaculum (above the ankle) and inferior extensor retinaculum (Y-shaped, over the dorsum of the foot) to prevent bowstringing during dorsiflexion. Order lateral to medial at the ankle: EDL, EHL, tibialis anterior (mnemonic: "Tom Has Do" — Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL from medial to lateral).

Lateral Compartment — Clinical Notes

Both fibularis tendons pass posterior to the lateral malleolus, held in place by the superior peroneal retinaculum. Peroneus brevis avulsion fracture at the 5th metatarsal styloid (pseudo-Jones or Jones fracture) occurs during forced inversion. Fibularis longus follows a unique course — it dips under the cuboid through the fibular groove of the cuboid to reach its plantar insertion, making it an important support of the transverse arch as well as an evertor.

Nerve supply (all three muscles): Tibial nerve, S1–S2

Gastrocnemius Exam Medial head: posterior medial femoral condyle; Lateral head: posterior lateral femoral condyle Via Achilles (calcaneal) tendon → posterior calcaneal tuberosity Plantarflexion of foot; flexion of knee Biarticular — most effective plantarflexor with knee extended. Contributes most to fast-speed propulsion.

Dominant postural/anti-gravity muscle. Predominately slow-twitch (Type I) fibres.
Plantaris Posterior lateral femoral condyle (just above lateral gastrocnemius head) Medial edge of Achilles tendon / posterior calcaneus Weak plantarflexion; weak knee flexion Small, vestigial muscle. Absent in ~7–10% of people. Long thin tendon frequently harvested for surgical grafts. "Tennis leg" = plantaris rupture.

Achilles Tendon

The Achilles (calcaneal) tendon is formed by the union of the gastrocnemius and soleus tendons (with plantaris contribution), inserting into the posterior calcaneal tuberosity. It is the thickest and strongest tendon in the body, transmitting forces up to 8× body weight during running. A hypovascular zone exists approximately 2–6 cm above the calcaneal insertion — the most common site of Achilles tendinopathy and rupture.

Tom, Dick and (a very) Nervous Harry — Tarsal Tunnel Mnemonic

The structures passing posterior to the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum (tarsal tunnel) from anterior to posterior: Tibialis posterior, DFDL (flexor Digitorum longus), posterior tibial Artery & veins, Nerve (tibial), HFL (flexor Hallucis Longus).
Mnemonic: "Tom, Dick And a Nervous Harry" (TP – FDL – Artery/vein – Nerve – FHL)

7. Intrinsic Foot Muscles (Slides 18–19)

Nerve supply: Medial plantar nerve (most layer 1 muscles; L4–L5) and Lateral plantar nerve (most deep muscles; S1–S2) — both terminal branches of the tibial nerve passing through the tarsal tunnel

The intrinsic muscles are arranged in 4 plantar layers (numbered from superficial) plus the dorsal intrinsics (EDB/EHB).
